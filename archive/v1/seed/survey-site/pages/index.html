Digital Twins: A Survey

Cities, infrastructure, populations and ecosystems

ArticleApplicationsEnginesWhole systemsEcological frontierDirectorySources

Advances in computation, geographic data and AI are expanding what we can simulate—and who can use the results. Transport planners can test road closures. Utilities can examine how electric vehicles and heat pumps will change demand. Fire agencies can estimate where a wildfire will spread. Communities can explore competing proposals for the places they live.

The digital-twin industry is growing around these applications: models of real places and systems, supplied with data from the world they represent. This survey asks who uses them, which engines and platforms underpin them, what they can do today, and how far they have progressed toward whole systems modeling. A further frontier is coming into view: using models to discover ways of living and producing within ecological systems whose interactions exceed our unaided ability to reason about them.

What is a digital twin?

A digital twin is a computational representation of a real object, place or system, linked to information about its real-world counterpart. The connection allows the model to be updated as conditions change and used to explore what might happen next—or what would happen if something were changed. The update interval depends on the application: traffic signals and long-term housing plans operate on different clocks. [1]

A traffic twin, for example, combines a road network with traffic counts, signal timings and vehicle movements. It can help an operator respond to congestion or let a planner experiment with a different junction layout. Tees Valley's traffic twin, developed with Aimsun, is already used at its traffic management centre to identify delayed buses and support changes to signal operation. [2] [3]

The wider simulation field includes the design of individual machines and operator training—for example, learning to control a tree harvester safely. The focus here is large-scale systems with public consequences: cities and regions, electricity and water networks, transport and crowds, watersheds and ecosystems. Their users include corporations, governments and citizens, often with different interests in the same place.

There are several names for this part of the field. Urban and territorial digital twins describe models organized around places. Infrastructure twins describe networks and assets. Cyber-physical-social systems explicitly include people and their behavior. Multiscale describes models that connect levels of detail, such as individual households and a regional electricity grid. These terms overlap; the survey uses the plain description “large-scale digital twins” rather than treating any one of them as a universal category. [4] [5]

This includes both operating twins and the simulation engines or data services used to build them. A general crop model, a traffic engine and a maintained twin of a particular region are different products, but all belong in an account of how these systems are assembled.

The audiences are visible in the applications. A utility wants to keep service running and choose investments. A local authority wants to compare transport, housing and environmental proposals. Residents may want to inspect a proposed road, explore an alternative, or understand which neighborhoods benefit. The same underlying model can support all three, provided each has an interface suited to its task.

The engines and platforms

The software market divides into geographic platforms, simulation engines and applications built for particular domains.

Esri and Bentley are central reference points for the geographic and infrastructure layer. ArcGIS organizes maps, spatial analysis and related data; Bentley iTwin integrates engineering and infrastructure information. Dassault Systèmes supplied the platform used for Virtual Singapore. These systems can give different teams a common representation of a place, its buildings and its assets. [30] [31] [32]

Simulation engines supply the behavior. AnyLogic supports agent-based, discrete-event and system-dynamics models. GAMA combines spatial data and agent-based modeling. MATSim models the travel choices and movements of many agents across transport networks; SUMO simulates vehicles, public transport and pedestrians. PyPSA provides power and energy-system modeling. Deltares and DHI supply water-modeling tools. A city project may use several of these engines. [14] [8] [9] [6] [7] [12] [15]

Specialist platforms package models around a job. Aimsun serves transport planners and traffic operators. Neara serves electricity networks. Urbanly and UrbanSim address development and land use. Technosylva combines fire-spread modeling, weather and asset information for wildfire operations and mitigation. These are often the products a buyer encounters before encountering the underlying mathematics. [10] [2] [17] [19] [16]

“Which engine?” is therefore partly a question about the model: roads, electrical networks and household decisions behave differently. The larger platform choice also concerns the data, interfaces and integrations a project needs. Appendix A separates those roles and lists representative systems.

What computation and AI are changing

Weather forecasting provides a concrete example. WeatherNext 3 uses satellite observations and training on weather-station measurements to produce hourly forecast updates, reaching five-kilometer resolution for selected variables. Results are distributed through data services and Google's products. That combination makes a computationally demanding model available to people who will never run the model themselves. [24] [33]

Earth Genome reduces a different barrier. Its Earth Index application lets a user label examples of a feature in satellite imagery, search for similar locations, inspect the results and export a map. Applications include locating mining activity and livestock facilities. In a September 2026 study, the team combined SSL4EO and AlphaEarth representations to improve cattle-facility detection. Reusable geospatial models are becoming ingredients in practical environmental investigations. [20] [34] [35]

Urbanly adds a conversational interface to urban simulation: the assistant helps set up and run experiments, while the simulation engine produces the numerical results. This changes how a planner can work with a model. It does not require the planner to build each experiment through code or a specialist modeling interface. [36]

These developments affect different costs: producing forecasts, extracting information from imagery, and setting up experiments. Together they make richer models available through ordinary data services and browser tools. Local data preparation and model calibration still account for work that a new interface cannot automatically eliminate.

Are there whole systems models yet?

There are already models that connect several domains. They differ in what they connect and how much detail they retain.

GCAM connects energy, water, agriculture and land, the economy, and climate. It can examine, for example, how a change in energy policy alters land and water demand. Developed over decades, it represents a substantial whole systems tradition that predates current digital-twin branding. Its purpose is to explore regional and global scenarios, rather than reproduce every building or household. [21]

PyPSA-Eur connects electricity to transport, buildings and industry. It can examine how an energy transition changes generation, networks, storage and demand across sectors. It is an open energy-system model, not a live operational twin of every connected asset. [37]

Urban models connect people and development. UrbanSim represents location choice and urban growth, with connections to transport modeling. Urbanly publishes comparisons between simulated and recorded housing development in parts of Los Angeles and New York. These models can address interactions that a geometric city model alone cannot: where households locate, what gets built, and how development responds to the modeled rules. [17] [18]

Connected infrastructure models address cascading failures. CReDO examines climate hazards and dependencies between networks. A flooded electricity asset can affect water pumping or communications, so the consequence extends beyond the damaged asset. Its September 2026 MVP showcase presents this approach to resilience planning; a final platform release is planned for 2028. [38] [39]

Whole systems modeling therefore exists in several forms: integrated models, linked domain models, and applications combining common data with specialist simulations. A comprehensive, routinely updated model of a city's physical, ecological, economic and social behavior remains a much larger undertaking. The examples above solve portions of that problem at different scales.

National and territorial twins

Virtual Singapore made the national-scale ambition visible: a shared 3D representation containing information about buildings, terrain and the city around them. GovTech's description illustrated uses such as assessing rooftop solar potential, flooding, wind and urban heat. Singapore's current geospatial programs continue to support applications including flood-risk assessment and solar-energy analysis. [40] [41]

The UK National Digital Twin Programme takes a broader infrastructure approach. Its published Isle of Wight demonstrators include electricity, housing retrofit and emergency planning. The energy demonstrator links network questions to electrification and renewable generation. The housing example brings together building characteristics, eligibility for retrofit funding and household vulnerability. Those examples show why social and policy information belongs alongside physical infrastructure. [11] [42] [43] [44]

These programs also expose the institutional scale of the task. A city's roads, electricity, water, land records and housing data are maintained by different organizations. Connecting their models requires agreements about sharing and updating that information, as well as software. DAFNI supplies research infrastructure for collaborative modeling; work such as Wenwen Li's at ASU addresses the discovery and integration of distributed geospatial data. [45] [46]

Making the models usable

Access already takes several forms. Helsinki publishes city-model data and a browser-based energy atlas. En-ROADS makes climate-policy scenarios available for public learning. Geodesignhub gives stakeholders a shared setting for developing and comparing spatial proposals. Earth Index puts environmental mapping tools in the hands of investigators. [20] [27] [28] [29]

A public planning interface could let someone move a proposed cycle route, compare traffic changes, and share the alternative with a council. Delivering that experience requires more than exposing an expert application's controls: the scenario must load quickly, the choices must be understandable, and the results must identify what changed. Whether residents can simply view a model or actually propose alternatives is an important product distinction.

The full stack runs from satellite imagery, sensors and administrative records through geographic data, simulation engines and linked models to the interface where somebody uses the result. A working application also needs a team to maintain those connections as roads, assets, populations and data sources change.

The industry has established suppliers for geography, transport, water and energy, alongside newer companies building environmental search, urban simulation and AI interfaces. Reusable connections between these tools would let more cities and watersheds draw on this accumulated modeling work.

Modeling beyond human intuition

The applications above often start with systems we have made explicit: a road network, an electricity grid, a zoning code, and a known set of controls. A more consequential frontier concerns systems whose organization is largely implicit. Climate, soils, water, organisms and human activity influence one another through relationships that we have only partly observed or formalized. Timothy Morton's term hyperobjects captures a related difficulty: phenomena distributed across so much space and time that we cannot encounter them as a comprehensible whole. [47]

Working with these systems means discovering more of their structure as we go. A useful model could help reveal a relationship we had overlooked, suggest an intervention we would not have considered, and identify an observation that would tell us whether it works. This extends the purpose of a twin from operating a system whose controls we understand to exploring possibilities within a system we are still learning to describe.

Agriculture makes the opportunity tangible. Uniform crops, standardized inputs and repeatable operations make production easier to organize. Machinery, labor, markets and policy all help explain that pattern. There is also a cost to managing ecological complexity: the best crop mixture for one soil, season and water regime may perform differently in another. Diversified farming already has a substantial history and evidence base. A 2020 synthesis found that diversification improved several ecosystem services and biodiversity without an average crop-yield penalty, although outcomes varied. Machine learning could extend farmers' and ecologists' ability to work with that diversity. [48]

Imagine planning a productive watershed as a changing mosaic of crops, grazing, trees, wetlands and habitat. A model could search combinations of planting, rotation, irrigation and restoration that would be impractical to compare by hand. The question becomes: which arrangements sustain food production and livelihoods while improving soil, water and biodiversity? This is a search through a fitness landscape with several objectives, delayed effects and changing conditions. There may be many useful sweet spots, and a gain in one outcome can come at another's expense.

In his essay Natural General Intelligence, Ryan Orbuch proposes a nature model that learns environmental state and dynamics across connected Earth systems, anticipates responses to intervention and helps decide what to measure next. It is a written proposal for a direction of research. The compelling idea is that greater intelligence could reveal opportunities to improve living systems that we have not yet recognized—or even known how to ask about. [49]

Fei-Fei Li's World Labs approaches world modeling through spatial intelligence. Its September 2026 Atlas announcement demonstrates scene reconstruction, generation and workflows for robotics simulation. AMD's agreement to acquire World Labs for approximately US$8.2 billion, reported by The Standard and confirmed in AMD's filing, shows the scale of investment entering this adjacent field. These developments suggest more of a model's structure can be learned from observations. Extending that capability to ecological responses over seasons and decades remains an open research challenge. [50] [51] [52]

Human intuition and machine reasoning would have complementary roles. Farmers, scientists and communities contribute experience, goals and constraints. Models propose combinations, estimate their consequences and identify where uncertainty changes the decision. Field trials and continued observation reveal which predictions survive contact with a particular place. The result could be a growing capacity to act intelligently within systems that remain too complex for any one person to understand in full. Appendix F follows this loop through the stack.

The goal is to leave a landscape with greater capacity to support life: healthier soils, more habitat, cleaner water and durable livelihoods. Carbon-negative activity belongs in that ambition, with emissions and removals considered across the whole activity rather than only within a field boundary. Living systems already regenerate many of the conditions on which production depends. Whole systems models could help us strengthen that regenerative capacity—and make its improvement an explicit measure of success.

Appendix B — From a question to a working twin

Consider a proposal to convert a traffic lane into a cycle lane. The transport model needs the road network, junctions, signal timings, public transport schedules and an estimate of travel demand. A scenario changes the available lanes and routes. The simulation estimates changes in traffic and journey times; other models may calculate emissions or accessibility. An interactive interface lets planners and residents compare proposals.

Several traditions contribute to these systems. Engineering simulation supplies physical models and operational practice. System dynamics supplies stocks, flows and feedback; Limits to Growth brought interacting environmental and economic scenarios to a wide audience in 1972. Agent-based modeling represents the decisions and interactions of households, travelers, firms and other actors. GIS supplies the geographic context in which those activities occur. Integrated assessment models such as GCAM connect human and Earth systems. [72] [21] [73] [74]

The participatory side also has a history: Engelbart's work on collective problem-solving, public scenario tools such as En-ROADS, and collaborative planning environments such as Geodesignhub. Today's browser interfaces and AI assistants extend this effort to make complex models usable beyond the team that built them. [27] [28] [75]

Appendix E — Research directions

Learned environmental representations. AlphaEarth and Prithvi provide reusable geospatial representations or models for tasks such as mapping and classification. Earth Genome shows how these components can be combined with a task-specific workflow. [34] [59] [35]

Nature models as a proposed direction. Ryan Orbuch's Natural General Intelligence essay connects learning environmental dynamics with choosing the next useful observation. Turning that idea into a working ecological application would require evidence that models predict responses to changed management, including conditions outside their training experience. [49]

Spatial world models. World Labs' Atlas combines images, video and 3D information to reconstruct scenes and support simulation. These are useful building blocks for representing places and physical interactions. The gap to a nature model includes biological processes, hydrology, nutrient cycles and long-term responses to intervention; those capabilities are not established by the Atlas demonstrations. World Labs announced its agreement to join AMD on September 28, 2026, with closing expected by year-end subject to approvals and other conditions. [52] [66]

Urban behavior and coupling. Urbanly, UrbanSim and research platforms such as GAMA address interactions among people, development and movement. Work on urban twins by Goodchild, Wenwen Li and colleagues examines how geographic representation, uncertainty and the purpose of a model fit together. [17] [18] [61] [9]

Shared public infrastructure. Destination Earth and DAFNI provide computational and modeling infrastructure. National and territorial programs face the additional task of keeping multiple organizations' data and models connected over time. [25] [46] [44]

Appendix F — A whole systems stack for stewardship

Consider a farming watershed where landholders want reliable production and income, healthier soils, less nutrient runoff and connected habitat. The following is an illustrative architecture for exploring that question, assembled from existing modeling capabilities and proposed research directions. It is not a description of a single available product.

Part of the stackWhat it would do in this watershed
Shared goals and local knowledgeFarmers, residents, ecologists and public agencies identify desirable outcomes, practical limits, land rights and interventions worth considering.
ObservationsCombine satellite imagery with weather, stream flow, soil samples, species surveys, management records and field observations. Decide which missing measurements would most affect the choice.
Connected modelsLink crop growth, soil and nutrient processes, hydrology, habitat and farm economics. DSSAT, InVEST and water-modeling tools cover parts of this problem; learned models could supply additional relationships or faster approximations. [22] [23] [15]
Search and experimentsExplore combinations of rotations, planting patterns, water management and restoration across several weather and price scenarios. Use the expensive simulations on the most promising or uncertain alternatives.
Human interpretationShow proposed changes on a map, explain the expected mechanisms and expose disagreements between models. Let participants add alternatives that the search did not suggest.
Field learningTrial selected changes, compare outcomes with expectations and update both the models and the management plan as the landscape changes.

What counts as a better outcome?

A useful interface would keep food production, livelihoods, water, biodiversity and net greenhouse-gas balance visible together. A single score can conceal an unacceptable loss: planting trees might increase carbon storage while damaging an existing grassland habitat. Participants need to see the alternatives and decide which trade-offs they will accept.

The search would therefore look for several promising arrangements under explicit constraints. This is multi-objective optimization: identifying options for which improving one objective requires giving up something elsewhere. The frontier changes with the model, the constraints and the conditions considered; a model's preferred arrangement is a candidate for investigation, not a discovered law of land use.

The agriculture evidence supports looking for joint gains, while showing why local testing matters. The diversification synthesis found average improvements in several services, but did not establish a universal climate benefit or a single best farming system. [48] A stewardship twin would help discover where a particular combination works, what sustains it and how neighboring places are affected.

Appendix G — Sensory landscapes

A street can move cars efficiently while being difficult to cross on foot. A landscape can contain suitable habitat while separating the animals that need it. Here, sensory landscape brings together sound, light, smell and the barriers that shape how people and other animals inhabit a place. It is a proposed direction for connected twins, drawing on existing work in sensory ecology, access and habitat connectivity.

Some pieces already exist. Walk Score measures walkability; Circuitscape models landscape connectivity, including wildlife corridors. Noise, lighting and dispersion engines can estimate conditions at particular locations. Connecting these to species-specific responses and human experience could let a planning team compare a road crossing, a quieter flight route, a dark corridor or a different lighting schedule.

The consequences can extend beyond immediate discomfort. Boyes and colleagues found fewer moth caterpillars beside lit hedgerows and grass margins than at matched unlit sites. Van Doren and colleagues observed a high-intensity light installation disrupting migrating birds. Serrano and Ochoa-Ochoa documented aircraft noise changing Pedregal frog calls and reducing chorus activity.

Bernie Krause’s account of a jet disrupting a frog chorus goes further: breaking the collective sound exposes individual callers to predators. That account is a useful illustration of a possible chain of effects; the Pedregal study measured vocal behavior, not increased predation. A coupled twin would need to test each link: disturbance, perception, behavior and ecological outcome.
