Ecosystem integrity is a way of describing the condition of a forest by its composition, structure and functions, and by how these maintain the state of the forest. The Primary Forests & Climate Program at Griffith University, which developed the assessment framework described here, defines it as the characteristics of an ecosystem that together describe its ability to achieve and maintain its "optimum operating state" under the prevailing environmental conditions and natural disturbances such as wildfire. A forest with a high level of integrity is self-organising and self-regenerating and does not rely on human management or inputs such as fertiliser. The term is also written "ecological integrity". It is assessed by three factors: composition (the dominant canopy tree species, the presence of species found only in mature forests, the absence of invasive weeds and feral animals), structure (vegetation density, amount of biomass, complexity of food webs) and processes (productivity, nutrient cycling, carbon uptake and storage), together with stability, which the Program splits into resistance, resilience and persistence. Risk of loss is the inverse of stability.

The argument that follows, made in a 2022 policy brief by Rogers, Mackey, Shestakova, Keith, Young, Kormos, DellaSala and others (also published in Frontiers in Forests and Global Change) and in a November 2023 brief by the IUCN World Commission on Protected Areas for COP28, is that a forest's carbon benefit rests on its biodiversity. Biodiversity, acted on by natural selection, is what gives a forest its adaptive capacity and stability, and stability is what keeps its carbon in place. The IUCN brief says a tropical forest that has been logged once stores about 35% less carbon than a primary one, a logged temperate forest may store 70% less, and a ton of carbon in a primary forest is not equivalent to a ton in a plantation, because the plantation is far more vulnerable and often unlikely to last more than a few decades. The 2022 brief ranks forest types by integrity: primary forest high on all three factors, secondary forest moderate depending on time since disturbance, production forest and agroforestry low to moderate, commercial plantations low. Both briefs argue that carbon accounting should distinguish forests by integrity. The 2022 brief says the existing rules, which report net changes in forest cover and treat all forests equally, encourage converting primary forest into production forest, and that the UN System of Environmental-Economic Accounting needs estimates of ecosystem condition for its accounts to be accurate; the IUCN brief says wider adoption of that standard would help. Wild Heritage, whose founder Cyril Kormos is an author on both briefs, worked with the Government of Costa Rica in 2018 to get text on primary forests and ecosystem integrity into Convention on Biological Diversity decisions, and the IUCN brief notes that the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework recognises ecosystem integrity in Goal A and Targets 1, 2 and 12.

For anyone modelling a forest the practical consequence is that area and carbon stock are not enough. The 2022 brief says there has been no agreed framework for assessing, mapping and reporting forest condition, and offers integrity as one; its ranking of forest types starts from the definition of primary forest used in the FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment: "Naturally regenerated forest of native tree species, where there are no clearly visible indications of human activities and the ecological processes are not significantly disturbed". A 2021 Program discussion paper for the FAO by Mackey, Skinner and Norman notes that although the definition is broadly accepted, studies show considerable variation in how countries apply it, which puts the comparability of their primary forest figures in question. Wild Heritage uses primary forest as an umbrella term covering old-growth, ancient and primeval forest and intact forest landscapes, and includes forest regenerating after wildfire and other natural disturbances.
