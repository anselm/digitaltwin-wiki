In an essay of 5 October 2026, Indy Johar argues against treating a bioregion as a bigger, more ecological territory with a new boundary. A boundary, however well drawn, still creates an inside and an outside, and consequences get pushed across the line. He proposes that a bioregion is made of bindings and knots. A binding is a relationship in which one party's condition affects another's viability. A knot is a place where many bindings shape each other, such as a farm, a town or a water system. His example is a water knot: soils, vegetation, groundwater, settlements, extraction works and the institutions governing them. It may reach upstream well beyond a catchment.

Under this view, institutions would hold obligations across knots. They would gather commitments from those whose actions jointly determine a condition of life. Responsibility would be shared out by causal contribution, exposure, benefit and capacity to respond. Lives that depend on the system but cannot make a claim would be given standing. Investment would be judged by its effect on the knots a site depends on, through something like a bioregional financing facility. Maps of these bindings would stay revisable, with sensing and machine assistance working alongside local knowledge.

A related strand is the rights-of-nature movement. A 2023 WWNO report describes Black and Indigenous organisers across the Mississippi basin seeking legal standing for the river. They follow Ecuador's 2008 constitution, which recognised the rights of nature, municipal ordinances in the United States, and cases brought on behalf of wild rice in Minnesota and salmon in Washington. Experts quoted in the report say a river crossing many states and tribal lands would be hard to give standing in court.
