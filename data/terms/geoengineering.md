The Royal Society's 2009 report *Geoengineering the climate*, from a study chaired by John Shepherd, defined geoengineering as "the deliberate large-scale manipulation of the planetary environment to counteract anthropogenic climate change".[^1,2] It split the methods in two. Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) takes CO2 out of the atmosphere, by enhancing uptake and storage on land or in the ocean or by engineered systems. Solar radiation management (SRM) reduces the sunlight the Earth absorbs, by deflecting it or by making the atmosphere, clouds or surface more reflective.[^2] The report grouped SRM methods by where they act: at the surface (land or ocean albedo), in the troposphere (clouds), in the upper atmosphere (the stratosphere and above) and in space.[^2]

## How the words have shifted

The two branches are now often handled apart. A pair of US National Academies reports in 2015 dropped the word geoengineering, which they found covers many activities unrelated to climate. They also rejected "climate engineering" as implying more precision and control than is possible. They used climate intervention instead, with one volume on carbon dioxide removal and one on reflecting sunlight.[^4] The IPCC's Sixth Assessment glossary has no entry for geoengineering. It defines carbon dioxide removal as human activities that remove CO2 from the atmosphere and durably store it, and solar radiation modification as measures not related to greenhouse gas mitigation that seek to limit warming, mostly by reducing the sunlight reaching the surface.[^3] Later assessments of the reflecting branch, from the National Academies in 2021, UNEP in 2023 and the Royal Society in 2025, are about solar methods alone.[^5,6,7]

## The solar methods

The IPCC glossary describes three. Stratospheric aerosol injection would put reflective aerosols such as sulphates into the lower stratosphere. Marine cloud brightening would spray sea salt aerosols into persistent low marine clouds to raise their droplet count and reflectivity. Cirrus cloud thinning would seed the upper troposphere with ice-nucleating particles to reduce cirrus, letting more longwave heat escape; it works on outgoing heat rather than sunlight but is often listed with the solar methods.[^3] The 2009 report also assessed brightening deserts and settlements, and space-based reflectors.[^2] It judged stratospheric aerosols the most promising of the solar methods it reviewed, because their effect would be spread more evenly than local methods, they could be deployed more readily than space-based ones and they would act within a year or two.[^2] The Royal Society's 2025 briefing, chaired by Keith Shine, concentrated on stratospheric aerosol injection and marine cloud brightening and called the first the more technically feasible to scale up and the better understood.[^7]

## The volcanic analogue

Stratospheric aerosol injection is modelled on large explosive eruptions, Mount Pinatubo in 1991 among them.[^12] NASA reports that Pinatubo, which erupted in the Philippines in June 1991, put about 15 million tons of sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, and that the average global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over the next 15 months.[^8] The 2009 report puts the peak global cooling at about 0.5 K, with regional effects that varied, among them a strengthening of the North Atlantic Oscillation.[^2] Aerosol in the stable stratosphere lasts on the order of years, so a single eruption affects surface climate for several years.[^2]

## How it is modelled

The [Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP)](/data-sources/geomip/) is an international effort that sets common experimental protocols for Earth system model simulations of SRM, run alongside the coordinated experiments of [CMIP6](/data-sources/cmip6/) and CMIP7.[^12] Ben Kravitz, Alan Robock and colleagues proposed it in 2011 with four standard experiments against CMIP5 scenarios, focused on stratospheric aerosols.[^9] In the first, G1, an abrupt quadrupling of CO2 is balanced by a uniform cut in incoming sunlight. Twelve models ran it. The cut offset most of the global warming and about 97 per cent of the Arctic sea ice loss, but left the tropics slightly cooler and the poles warmer than before industrialisation, and reduced rainfall in some tropical regions. All the models agreed that a uniform dimming could not restore regional and global temperatures and the intensity of the water cycle at the same time.[^10]

GeoMIP6, designed for CMIP6, added experiments on extreme events, on SRM as one part of a wider response and on cirrus thinning, along with a testbed for trial designs.[^11] The protocol for CMIP7, published by Daniele Visioni and co-authors in September 2026, sorts its experiments into preparatory runs that diagnose how a model responds to injection at single latitudes, a core Tier 1 and a flexible Tier 2.[^12] The four Tier 1 experiments branch in 2035 from the CMIP7 medium-low emissions scenario and aim to hold global mean surface temperature at 1.5 °C above preindustrial. Three use stratospheric aerosols, one of them injecting at high latitude and low altitude, and one uses marine cloud brightening, with three ensemble members suggested for each.[^12] Injection rates are set by a feedback control algorithm that adjusts them to hold the target temperature.[^12] Each model's preindustrial baseline is taken from its own 2020 to 2039 average, because models disagree about preindustrial temperature.[^12]

### Termination

If sustained SRM stopped abruptly while greenhouse gases stayed high, temperatures would rise quickly toward the warmer climate the gases imply. The 2009 report called this the termination problem.[^2] The Royal Society's 2025 briefing put the rise at 1 to 2 °C within a couple of decades.[^7] The CMIP7 protocol includes a Tier 1 experiment, G7-1.5K-SAI-End, that stops injection at the end of 2084 and runs on for at least 15 years.[^12] The briefing expects such a rise to fall hardest on ecosystems and people that cannot adapt quickly.[^7] How fast a system is pushed, and not only how far, is also a theme of work on [tipping points](/terms/tipping-points/).

### Regional effects

Global averages hide the differences that matter for a place. In idealised studies the benefits of SRM are not spread evenly by geography, and models differ in parts of their projected response. Studies that assume uniform deployment also leave out the possibility of injecting unevenly across hemispheres.[^12] Modelling evidence that globally coordinated SRM would lower average temperature is robust, according to the 2025 briefing, but confidence in models' ability to predict regional impacts is much lower. Concentrating injection in one hemisphere or along the equator could alter rainfall unevenly.[^7] Stratospheric aerosols also bear on ozone: the 2022 Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion, made under the [Montreal Protocol](/mandates/montreal-protocol/), included a new chapter on the impacts of stratospheric aerosol injection on the stratosphere and ozone.[^17]

## Assessments

The 2009 Royal Society report recommended a ten-year UK research programme of about £10 million a year and preferred CDR over SRM.[^1] The National Academies' *Reflecting Sunlight* (2021) recommended a US transdisciplinary research programme run with other nations, governed by a code of conduct, a public registry of research and permitting for outdoor experiments.[^5] UNEP's *One Atmosphere* (February 2023), from an expert panel it convened, concluded that SRM is not ready for large-scale deployment, needs much more research on its risks and benefits, and cannot replace rapid cuts in emissions.[^6] The Royal Society's 2025 briefing found that SRM could lower global temperature if deployed globally but should not be the main policy response to climate change.[^7]

## Governance and field tests

The Convention on Biological Diversity held its tenth Conference of the Parties in Nagoya in October 2010.[^14] Its decision X/33 includes guidance for Parties to ensure "that no climate-related geo-engineering activities that may affect biodiversity take place" until there is an adequate scientific basis. It makes an exception for small-scale scientific research in controlled settings.[^13] A footnote gives a provisional scope: technologies that deliberately reduce solar insolation or increase carbon sequestration, excluding carbon capture and storage at fossil fuel sources.[^13]

Two outdoor tests were stopped in 2024. Harvard's Stratospheric Controlled Perturbation Experiment (SCoPEx) would have released a few kilograms of material, such as calcium carbonate or sulphuric acid, from a balloon-borne platform and measured the plume. A hardware test flight planned from Kiruna, Sweden, in 2021 was cancelled on its advisory committee's recommendation after opposition from the Saami Council and environmental groups, and on 18 March 2024 the principal investigator, Frank Keutsch, said he was no longer pursuing the experiment.[^15] In June 2024 the city council of Alameda, California, voted five to none to end a test of a cloud-brightening sprayer on the museum ship USS Hornet, run by the University of Washington and the nonprofit SilverLining. City staff had found that it posed no measurable risk to health or wildlife.[^16]
