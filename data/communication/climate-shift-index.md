The Climate Shift Index is a daily map from Climate Central that gives each place on Earth a score for how much climate change has altered the odds of that day's temperature there.[^1,2] People feel the weather where they live, not the global average, and the index is meant to connect the two.[^1] A reader opens the map, picks a day or a run of days, and sees the world coloured by score. They can switch to the plain temperature or to how far it is from normal, look up a city, and copy a link or a screenshot.[^2]

The scale runs from -5 to +5.[^1] A score of 3 means a temperature now happens at least three times as often as it would in a world without human-caused carbon pollution.[^1] A score of 5 means at least five times; such a temperature would be very hard to meet without climate change.[^1] Negative scores mean a temperature has become rarer, as cold days have.[^1] Climate Central gives an example: if 90°F used to come about one June day a year somewhere and now comes about three, a 90°F June day there scores 3.[^1] The index does not say climate change caused the heat, only how much it changed the odds.[^1] If either of the two observation-based methods or the average of the model-based methods disagrees on whether climate change played a part, the score is 0.[^1]

Two estimates are averaged. One compares 22 climate models run with and without historical greenhouse gas emissions.[^1] The other, observation-based, uses the past 30 years of daily temperatures and how strongly the local temperature responds to the rise in global average temperature, to work out how often a temperature would come without that rise.[^1] The map draws on NOAA's GFS weather forecasts, so it can show tomorrow and the day after as well as today.[^2] The method was set out in 2022 in "A multi-method framework for global real-time climate attribution" by Daniel M. Gilford, Andrew Pershing, Benjamin H. Strauss, Karsten Haustein and Friederike E. L. Otto, published open access in Advances in Statistical Climatology, Meteorology and Oceanography;[^4] Climate Central launched the index the same year.[^1] The paper describes one model-based and two observation-based methods, and its worked example is daily highs in Phoenix, Arizona, in July 2016.[^4] Gilford, Pershing and Strauss are also authors of Climate Central's [paper on extreme water levels](/articles/climate-central-extreme-water-levels/).[^7]

Versions now cover ocean surface temperatures, humid heat and the wind strength of tropical cyclones.[^1,3] Climate Central uses the index in alerts ahead of heat waves and in reports, and its resource page shows meteorologists and science communicators using it on air.[^3] The city pages of [Climate Local](/communication/climate-local/) count each city's days this year that scored 2 or higher.[^6]

For broadcast graphics, the daily maps of high and low temperature scores are published as KML files at fixed links that update each day; the page says the link can be pasted "into a Max scene" to get daily-updating data.[^2] Climate Central's terms of use allow any non-commercial use, commercial news use by media organizations and educational use by accredited educational bodies, with credit and, online, a link.[^5] They forbid bulk downloading and scraping, and other commercial use, in particular for financial products or investments, needs written permission.[^5]

Each score is one number for one place and one day, short enough for a forecaster to say alongside the forecast.
