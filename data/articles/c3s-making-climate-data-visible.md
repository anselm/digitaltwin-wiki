The author has been the C3S climate data visualiser at ECMWF for three years and writes in the first person. The essay appeared on 23 September 2026. Its graphics go into the C3S Monthly Climate Bulletins, the Global Climate Highlights and the European State of the Climate (ESOTC) reports.[^1]

The service tracks variables such as air temperature, soil moisture, sea surface temperature, precipitation and sea ice extent every day. For each chart the visualiser tries several chart types, colour palettes and levels of detail, and keeps talking with the scientists until the graphic says one clear thing.[^1]

Two examples are given. One is an infographic of glacier loss in the Alps for ESOTC 2024, made with glacier experts at the University of Zurich and WGMS.[^1,2] The other is a stacked bar chart showing that every day of 2023, 2024 and 2025 was more than 1 °C above pre-industrial levels.[^1] A ridgeline plot (curves stacked one above another) of global daily temperature anomalies, published in the Global Climate Highlights report in January 2026, was widely shared by journalists and policy makers.[^1]

A companion piece on the ESOTC 2024 report sets out the production rules:[^2]

- style guidelines fixed at the start of drafting
- labels and annotations so each chart stands alone when shared
- several rounds of review with scientists
- responsive charts for mobile
- a graphics gallery of about 130 images, each with a one-click download of its processed data[^2]

The glacier map was drawn in QGIS and finished in Adobe Illustrator.[^2]

The essay ends on Visualising Climate, a conference on climate and environmental data visualisation. Copernicus and ECMWF co-host it in Bologna on 4 to 6 November 2026.[^1] The piece is about [presentation](/presentation/): how a model's output becomes something a reader can take in.
