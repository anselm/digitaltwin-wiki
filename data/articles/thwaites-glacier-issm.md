Ice-sheet models have struggled to reproduce the retreat of Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica.[^1] This paper, published on 12 October 2026, asks whether the answer lies at the grounding line, where ice stops resting on bedrock and starts to float.[^1] The six authors are Mattia Poinelli, Eric Rignot, Hélène Seroussi, Joshua Cuzzone, Tyler Pelle and Eric Larour, at UCLA, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, UC Irvine, Dartmouth and Scripps.[^1]

Most models treat that line as a sharp boundary, but the authors represent it as a grounding zone a few kilometres wide, inside which seawater gets beneath ice that is still grounded and melts it from below.[^1] They build this into the Ice-sheet and Sea-level System Model (ISSM) and start it from a mid-1990s state.[^1] They calibrate it against observed surface velocity, ice thickness and grounding-line positions from 1996 to 2023 by a Bayesian approach, which weighs how likely each set of model settings is, given the observations.[^1]

By the authors' account, nearly three decades of observed retreat, thinning and acceleration can only be reproduced with grounding zones 2-5 km wide.[^1] Runs without a grounding zone are four times less likely to reproduce the historical retreat than runs with one a few kilometres wide.[^1] Run forward, the calibrated model has the glacier detaching from stabilising seafloor ridges as early as 2038 and gives up to 30 ± 6 mm of global mean sea-level rise by 2100 without added climate warming, roughly double earlier projections.[^1]

The ISSM model and the grounding-zone parameterisation are on GitHub and Zenodo, and the code and model output to reproduce the runs are on Zenodo.[^1] The velocity, elevation-change and grounding-line observations are each available through a cited reference.[^1] The article is open access under CC BY 4.0.[^1]
