Warming is expected to speed up the water cycle. What that does to the water available on land is less certain.[^1] This paper reports what happened in 2023 and 2024, which it calls the record-warm period. Across the world, the flow of rivers and the water held in lakes and reservoirs fell at the same time, by more than in any period since 1991.[^1]

This entry is written from the abstract alone. The article is behind a paywall.

## What was observed

- **Streamflow and stored surface water declined together**, at global scale, during 2023 and 2024.[^1]
- **It was the largest reduction since 1991.**[^1]
- **South America accounted for 70.4 per cent of the global decline in streamflow**, give or take 4.2.[^1]
- **Humid regions were not spared.** The authors conclude that extreme warming "can induce abrupt water losses even in humid regions".[^1]

## How it was measured

Two kinds of record, per the abstract: streamflow observations from 150 major river basins, combined with a model by assimilation, and satellite data for more than 1.2 million water bodies.[^1] The abstract does not name the datasets or the model. The comparison with 1991 implies a record at least that long.

## What drove it

The authors attribute the drop to two things happening at once in the oceans: an El Niño in the Pacific and unusual warmth in the tropical North Atlantic. Together these produced sinking air over large areas, carried moisture away, and deepened drought.[^1] They call it "compound ocean–atmosphere forcing".[^1]

## What a model would have to reproduce

This section is the wiki's reading.

For a model of a river basin, the finding is a test with a date on it. Run with the weather of 2023 and 2024, the model should lose flow and storage on something like the observed scale. For a basin in South America the test is severe.

It is also a relationship to carry. Dry years in distant basins were not independent of each other in this period: one pattern in two oceans moved them together. A model that draws each basin's dry years separately will understate how often many fail at once, which matters to anything that crosses basins, such as food trade, hydropower shared over a grid, or a river that supplies several countries.

The abstract does not say how much of the decline was the two ocean patterns and how much the long-term warming beneath them, or how quickly flow recovered. Those are the first things to look for in the full text. The wiki's [Vitals](/vitals/) page carries the Niño 3.4 index and sea surface temperature, the two series nearest to the cause named here.
