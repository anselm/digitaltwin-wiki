A flood map, a warming chart or a simulated fire season is usually published on the assumption that people who see a risk clearly will act on it. Researchers who have measured what people feel and intend after seeing such things report a less dependable result. Some people become more willing to act. Some withdraw, and in one study residents shown their own house inside a projected flood zone came away less worried than residents shown nothing.[^7]

This article collects what ten studies measured. Only four of them put a map or a model in front of people. The rest are surveys of how people feel about climate change and experiments with written messages, and they are included because they are the evidence most often cited when someone says a model's output is too frightening or not frightening enough.

## Which models this evidence is about

A model of a place is built to change what happens there, and it can do so by two routes. In the first, software acts on the model's output without a person deciding each time. A farm's model of its soil moisture can set the watering schedule, in the way a thermostat acts on a thermometer. A model used like this is tested every time it acts: its prediction is compared with what the sensors report next, and the result (water used, crop yield) is compared with running without it. The [guide to checking a model against what happened](/guides/check-it-against-what-happened/) covers that test.

In the second route, people look at the output and then argue, vote, buy, build or move. Here the model works only through what those people come to believe and decide, and that is much harder to test. Every study in this article concerns the second route. A single model could serve both. A flood model, for example, could close a barrier automatically and also be shown at a public meeting.

Two other differences between models bear on how people respond, and the studies cover them unevenly.

The first is how soon the answer is needed. Some questions are slow, such as whether it is safe to build on a floodplain or in a forest that burns. Some are fast, such as where to place sandbags tonight or where to send fire crews in the next hour. All of the studies below are about slow questions: sea level in 2100, the climate over a lifetime. None tested a warning or a live map during an emergency. The one finding that touches the difference is from the fear-appeal research, where frightening messages worked better when they recommended a single action than when they recommended a repeated one.[^4]

The second is the area a model covers: a neighbourhood, a watershed or region, or the whole planet. Here the studies disagree in a way that matters. In the 63-country experiment, the message that most strengthened belief in climate change was the one that made it feel close to the reader in place and time.[^6] In the flood map study, the closest possible picture, the reader's own house, lowered their concern.[^7]

## Eco-paralysis

Eco-paralysis is the name for a response in which a person who is afraid for the environment does nothing about it. A 2023 paper by Matteo Innocenti and colleagues attributes the term to the philosopher Glenn Albrecht, writing in 2011, and describes it as "a passive state of behavioural stasis" that is sometimes mistaken for apathy.[^2] The difference matters to anyone presenting a model. An apathetic viewer has not been reached. A paralysed viewer has been reached and has concluded that nothing they do will count.

It is measured with an eleven-item questionnaire. People rate their agreement with statements such as "The changes in the global climate are so profound that they can no longer be stopped" and "I try not to think about climate change issues."[^1] The scale therefore mixes two things: a belief that the problem cannot be solved, and a habit of avoiding it.

Innocenti and colleagues surveyed 394 adults in Italy and found that climate anxiety was related to environmental behaviour in two opposite ways at once.[^2] More anxious people reported more environmental behaviour. More anxious people also reported lower self-efficacy, meaning less confidence in their own ability to handle difficulties, and lower self-efficacy went with less environmental behaviour.[^2] The authors read the second path as the route to eco-paralysis, although this study did not measure eco-paralysis itself.[^2] A second paper from the same group, which validated the Italian eco-paralysis questionnaire on 150 people, reports that worry and education each weakened the link between anxiety and paralysis.[^3]

## Distress, and annoyance at other people's distress

The largest study of eco-paralysis found here is a survey of 1,603 Americans aged 13 to 22, taken in autumn 2024 by Brianne Suldovsky, Sarah Vaala and Matthew Lapierre.[^1] It asked how distressed each person was about climate change, how willing they were to act alone (recycling, saving energy) and with others (voting, petitions, volunteering), and gave them the eco-paralysis questionnaire.[^1]

Distress predicted both outcomes. The more distressed a young person was, the more willing they said they were to act, and the higher they scored on eco-paralysis.[^1] The study does not show what decides which way a distressed person goes. It does identify a second feeling that was the strongest predictor of paralysis and also predicted lower willingness to act. The authors call it climate contempt, and they measured it with four statements, among them "I am bored of hearing about climate change" and "I am annoyed by the constant publicity around climate change."[^1] They note that this measures annoyance at coverage and at other people's alarm, and may not be contempt as the term is defined elsewhere.[^1]

Where people had learned about climate change made little difference. Learning from social media predicted nothing. Learning from mass media and from friends, family and teachers predicted willingness to act with others, weakly, and no source predicted paralysis.[^1]

The study has limits that the authors state. It is a single survey, so it cannot show that contempt causes paralysis. It records what people said they were willing to do, not what they did. The sample was recruited from people who take paid online surveys and is not nationally representative.[^1] It also tested no map, model or message, so it cannot say that any particular way of showing a risk produces contempt.

## Fear, and something to do about it

The older question is whether frightening people works at all. A 2015 meta-analysis by Melanie Tannenbaum and colleagues pooled 127 articles and 248 samples, about 27,000 people, in which one group received a message designed to frighten and a comparison group did not.[^4] On average the frightening messages moved attitudes, intentions and behaviour in the intended direction, and the authors report finding no circumstances in which they backfired.[^4] The messages worked better when they included what the authors call efficacy statements, which tell the reader that there is something they can do and that it works.[^4] The abstract does not say how many of the studies concerned environmental risks, so how far the result transfers to climate is not established here.

Two studies of climate imagery by Saffron O'Neill and Sophie Nicholson-Cole reached a narrower conclusion in 2009. Fearful images of climate change caught attention, but, in the authors' words, fear was "generally an ineffective tool for motivating genuine personal engagement", and images tied to people's everyday concerns engaged them more.[^5]

A 2024 experiment led by Madalina Vlasceanu tested eleven kinds of message on 59,440 people in 63 countries and measured four outcomes, one of which cost the participant real effort: a tedious screen task that earned a planted tree for each page completed.[^6] One of the eleven messages was written in what the authors call a "doom and gloom" style. It raised willingness to share climate information on social media more than any other message, by 12.1% over the control group.[^6] It also reduced the number of trees people earned, and among people who began with low belief in climate change it reduced support for climate policy.[^6] No message of any kind increased tree planting.[^6] The authors point out that the messages which reduced tree planting were also the longest to read, so some of the reduction may come from participants running out of time or patience.[^6] All of the effects were small.[^6]

Taken together, these studies do not support a rule that alarming output should be softened. They support two narrower statements. Alarm with nothing attached to it produces cheap responses, such as sharing, more reliably than costly ones. And a message does better when it also shows that something can be done.

## When the thing shown was a map

Matto Mildenberger and colleagues surveyed residents who live near the line that sea-level rise is projected to reach by 2100, in the San Francisco Bay Area, Palm Beach County in Florida, Norfolk in Virginia and Ocean County in New Jersey.[^7] Some respondents were shown an aerial map of projected flooding in their own census tract with their address marked on it. The others answered the same questions without a map.[^7]

The map lowered concern. People whose address fell just outside the projected flood zone rated the likely harm to themselves half a point lower, on a four-point scale, than people who saw no map. People whose address fell inside the zone rated it a third of a point lower.[^7] The map did not change how much harm people expected for their community or for future generations.[^7] Residents inside the zone did become more supportive of flood insurance and flood defences, though not of costlier measures such as limiting development.[^7]

A further group in California was told how flooding of roads and transit would lengthen commutes. That information raised concern about sea-level rise, both for the respondent personally and for others, and raised support for flood defences.[^7] The authors conclude that mapping an individual's own risk may not build support for adaptation, and that a way of presenting risk should be tested before it is released.[^7] This account rests on the authors' own summary; the paper was not read for this article, and the summary does not give the sample size.

An earlier and smaller study points the other way for a different audience. David Retchless showed college students an interactive map of sea-level rise in Sarasota, Florida. Their sense of the risk rose after using it, and it rose more among students who lived far from Sarasota than among those nearby, and more among those who began doubtful about climate change than among those already alarmed.[^8]

## When people worked the model themselves

The [World Climate Simulation](/communication/world-climate-simulation/) is a role-play of a United Nations climate negotiation in which each round of pledges is entered into a climate model and the group sees the resulting temperature. Juliette Rooney-Varga and colleagues surveyed participants before and after 39 sessions in eight countries, 2,042 people in all, of whom 42% returned both surveys in usable form.[^9]

After playing, participants scored higher on knowledge of climate change, on a measure the authors call urgency (worry, fear, alarm, anger and personal importance) and on hope, and they reported more intention to act.[^9] The gains in intention went with the gains in urgency and hope and not with the gains in knowledge.[^9] The result that bears on eco-paralysis is that fear and hope rose together. The authors offer a possible reason, which they label as speculation: the game does not hand people a frightening message, and participants "feel more fearful as they experience for themselves the simulated consequences of their own decisions".[^9]

The study's limits are of the same kind as the survey's. There was no comparison group that received a lecture or a chart instead of the game. The outcome is stated intention, and the authors say that whether participants follow through remains to be assessed. A little over half of the participants had chosen to attend.[^9] An initial evaluation of the Climate Action Simulation, the companion game played on the [En-ROADS](/communication/en-roads/) model, reports similar gains in knowledge and engagement from surveys and a focus group.[^10]

## What a builder can take from this

What follows is this article's reading of the studies above. None of them tested a dashboard or a model of a particular place, and no such study was found in this search.

Test the piece on its audience before it is released. In two of the studies a reasonable expectation turned out wrong: that a map of someone's own house would raise their concern, and that some message would raise effortful action. The [guide to putting a model in front of its users](/guides/put-it-in-front-of-its-users/) recommends sitting with the first users, and these studies suggest also asking those users what they now think can be done.

Show the available actions beside the risk. This is the efficacy finding from the fear research, and it is consistent with the self-efficacy result in Italy and with the rise of hope alongside urgency in the World Climate sessions.

Describe a consequence in terms of an ordinary day. Commute delays moved people who were not moved by a flood line across their street, and O'Neill and Nicholson-Cole found the same for images tied to everyday concerns.

Let people change the inputs where that is possible. The evidence for this is the weakest of the four, because the game studies had no comparison group, but they are the only studies here that recorded fear and hope increasing together.

The questionnaires are available to anyone who wants to measure this for their own work. Suldovsky and colleagues print their full survey, including the eleven eco-paralysis statements and the four contempt statements, in an appendix published under a Creative Commons Attribution licence.[^1] A builder can give the same statements to a test audience before and after a session and see which way the scores move.

## What is not known

Almost every outcome above is something a person said they would do. Only the tree-planting task measured effort actually spent, and it gave the most discouraging result. The samples are mostly from the United States and Europe, with the 63-country experiment as the exception. No study here follows people for more than the length of a session, so nothing is known from them about whether paralysis or resolve lasts. None concerns a fast question, where a person must act on a model's output within hours. And none examines the setting this wiki is mostly about, in which a council, a utility or a neighbourhood group is shown a model of its own place and has to decide something.
