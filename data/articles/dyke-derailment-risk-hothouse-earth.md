In 2018 Will Steffen and colleagues drew the Earth system as a ball on a landscape: human pressure has pushed it out of the Holocene valley towards a threshold beyond which feedbacks in the climate itself, not emissions, would carry it on to a "Hothouse Earth" several degrees warmer. Staying this side of the threshold takes deliberate stewardship.[^1,2] James Dyke, Laurie Laybourn and Jesse Abrams of the University of Exeter's Global Systems Institute argue that this picture has a structural gap. It treats humanity as a force acting on the system from outside. Humanity is a part of the system, and the capacity to steward it can be damaged by the very changes it is meant to steer.[^1]

## The argument

They call the gap derailment risk: the possibility that escalating Earth system disruption weakens the social, political and economic conditions needed to sustain mitigation, adaptation and restoration.[^1] The capacity to act is broken into three kinds of agency. Work agency is the energy, materials and infrastructure to build, remove carbon and restore ecosystems. Economic agency is the finance to direct at that work. Political agency is the ability to allocate the funds and pass the laws.[^1] Each is exposed to the climate it is meant to fix. The paper's standing example is the fiscal "doom loop" already visible in low-income countries: a disaster forces emergency borrowing, debt service crowds out climate investment, exposure to the next shock grows.[^1] Climate risk is already lowering sovereign credit ratings, and tropical cyclones are leaving debt burdens that constrain budgets for decades.[^1] The authors say this risk is under-theorised and under-addressed in the literature on Earth system governance, the field the [Earth System Governance Project](/organizations/earth-system-governance-project/) organises.[^1]

The word [polycrisis](/terms/polycrisis-and-metacrisis/) is one of the paper's keywords, and it is used in the technical sense of Lawrence and colleagues' 2024 framework: crises entangled by common stresses, domino effects and inter-systemic feedbacks. Derailment risks are a class of polycrisis dynamics, the ones that reduce the ability to deal with climate-driven impacts and their drivers.[^1]

The authors redraw the landscape with two axes instead of one: global temperature and governance effectiveness. Social feedback is not a second state variable but a parameter that deforms the landscape. As governance degrades, the Hothouse basin deepens and the threshold moves closer, so there are two thresholds, the purely biophysical one of 2018 and an earlier one that the coupled system can cross with less warming.[^1] The deformation is not symmetric: trust and institutions are easier to destroy than to rebuild, so stewardship applied before social destabilisation is worth more than the same effort after.[^1] Stabilising loops exist too. Extreme weather has at times raised political will, and in Europe climate impacts have sometimes moved votes towards decarbonisation.[^1] The question is which kind of loop dominates.

## The framework

Three stages: map the socio-biophysical feedbacks and assess the risk; act in advance to build resilience; and prepare responses to be delivered under duress. The worked example is food. The 2010 Russian heat wave and wheat export ban fed price spikes that helped intensify unrest across the Middle East and North Africa; the 2022 disruption of Ukrainian grain reached import-dependent countries in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia; Sri Lanka's 2022 crisis had fuel and food prices as its proximate triggers.[^1] For the third stage the authors describe "duress ready" policy: automatic stabilisers that act without new votes, pre-committed triggers and responses, implementation spread across many actors so it is hard to reverse, and rules ready in advance to stop maladaptive moves such as expanding farming onto carbon-rich land when yields fall.[^1] They argue the aim should be to minimise derailment risk rather than to optimise economic cost, and that the two are often in tension.[^1]

## What it asks of models

This is where the paper reaches the tools on this site. The shared socioeconomic pathways that integrated assessment models run assume agency: SSP2, the "middle of the road", assumes gradual institutional strengthening, and SSP3 assumes fragmentation but not as a consequence of climate impacts.[^1] The authors call for a new, dynamic class of pathways in which agency is a function of social conditions and biophysical shocks, so it can fall as well as rise, and they concede that parameterising that function will be hard and suggest sensitivity analysis as the first step.[^1] It is the same point the social-ecological tradition has made about coupled human-Earth systems, carried to planetary scale; the paper cites Donges and colleagues' call to "close the loop" between social science and Earth system science.[^1] An [Earth system model](/terms/earth-system-model/) has the carbon cycle in the loop and not the treasury. The [tipping points](/terms/tipping-points/) entry covers the early-warning side.

The paper is a perspective: no new data were created or analysed, and the authors describe their mapping as structural rather than quantified.[^1] Its last sentence rejects "the notion that there will come a day when what we do no longer matters."[^1]
