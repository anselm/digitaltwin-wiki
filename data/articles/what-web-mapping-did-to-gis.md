In the middle of the 2000s the making of maps passed, within about four years, from a profession with its own software to anyone who could write a web page, and then to anyone who could walk a street with a GPS receiver. This article sets out what happened, who in the San Francisco Bay Area was doing the work, and what researchers later said the change did and did not achieve. It then asks what in that history applies to models of places, which may be at a similar point now.

## Before and after 2005

A geographic information system (GIS) is software for storing, analysing and drawing data about places. Until the mid-2000s it was specialist software used by trained staff in agencies, utilities and consultancies. A reporter at the first Where 2.0 conference, which O'Reilly Media held in San Francisco on 29 and 30 June 2005, wrote that the people in the room came from "disparate communities that have rarely mixed in the past, including geographers, cartographers, geographic information system (GIS) specialists, telecommunications engineers, Web developers, and hackers".[^1] About 500 attended.[^1] He gave the conference's theme as this: "the old business of GIS is about to be disrupted big-time by the arrival of Web-based technologies that put the power to access, explore, create, and enhance digital maps into the hands of average people".[^1]

The events behind that sentence came close together. Google announced Google Maps on 8 February 2005, with a map that could be dragged without waiting for a new image to load.[^2] On 29 June, the first day of the conference, it released a programming interface that let any web developer put that map on their own site, and Yahoo said at the conference that it would release one for its own maps.[^1,3] Steve Coast had registered openstreetmap.org in August 2004 and entered the project's first street that December; its foundation was set up in August 2006.[^4] [OpenStreetMap](/data-sources/openstreetmap-osm/) meant that the base map itself, and not only what was drawn on top of it, could be made by volunteers.

A 2008 review in Geography Compass said the landscape of internet mapping had "changed dramatically since 2005", listed the new words that had arrived with it (mash-ups, crowdsourcing, neogeography) and called the whole a step change from the previous generation of internet mapping.[^5] In 2007 the geographer [Michael Goodchild](/people/michael-goodchild/) gave the public's contribution a name in the title of a paper, "Citizens as sensors: the world of volunteered geography".[^6]

## Who was doing the work

The meetings were part of it. WhereCamp was an unconference, a meeting whose agenda is agreed by the participants on the day, that followed each Where 2.0 and at first involved sleeping over; others took the name and ran their own in other cities.[^7] Anselm Hook, who maintains this wiki, was one of its co-founders.[^8] He recalls the mood of those years this way: "we were all fairly excited to have participatory maps at all. It was novel."

Two Bay Area organisations show the two directions the work took. Stamen Design, founded by Eric Rodenbeck in San Francisco in 2001, is a studio for data visualisation and cartography that holds that "utility and beauty share an equal footing".[^9] Its Cabspotting drew the live positions of San Francisco taxis from their GPS data, in a project with the Exploratorium. Stamen describes it as among the first uses of real-time data in a visualisation on the web, and it is now in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art.[^10] GreenInfo Network, a non-profit that began in the mid-1990s by mapping the region's open space, took the other direction: maps, data and web applications for groups working on the environment, public health, education and social justice. It says it has worked with more than 1,000 organisations since 1996.[^11] Its account of its own purpose is that the maps were also a way "to connect people and groups together".[^11]

A second tradition in the same region made pictures of systems without any geography in them. The Grove Consultants International, founded by David Sibbet in 1977, describes itself as among the first in the field of organisation development to use visual techniques, and its practice is visual facilitation: drawing during a meeting so that the group works from one shared picture.[^12] Global Business Network, founded in Berkeley in 1987 by Peter Schwartz, Jay Ogilvy, Stewart Brand and others, sold scenario planning, which its practitioners distinguish from forecasting: a forecast extends present trends, and a scenario exercise explores several plausible futures and what each would mean for a decision.[^13] The firm was bought by Monitor Group in 2000 and ceased to operate in 2013.[^13] The wiki's [Scenarios](/scenarios/) shelf uses the word in that sense, and the entry on [Anticipatory Democracy](/media/anticipatory-democracy/) covers the public form of the same practice a decade earlier.

## What the change did not do

The claim made at the time was that mapping had been democratised. In 2013 Mordechai Haklay, who had co-written the 2008 review, examined that claim. His abstract says that once the values built into the technology are looked at, "neogeography becomes far less exciting and transformative", and that there remained "a separation between a technological elite and a wider group of uninformed, labouring participants who are not empowered through the use of the technology".[^14] He allows that the lower barriers did bring "some increased level of democratisation", and says that fulfilling the promise "requires careful implementation that takes into account social and political aspects".[^14] This article has read only the abstract of that paper.

So the record is mixed. A person who added streets to a shared map did not thereby gain any say over what was built on them. What the period did establish was durable in a narrower way: an open base map that anyone can use, the expectation that a map on a screen can be moved and queried by the person looking at it, and a body of designers who treat a map as something to be read by the public.

## From a picture of a place to a model of one

Everything above concerns description: where things are, drawn well and shared widely. The older ambition was to compute what a place will do next. SimCity, released in 1989, is the form in which most people met it. Its designer, Will Wright, turned to Jay Forrester's 1969 book [Urban Dynamics](/media/urban-dynamics/) to make his cities behave, and the historian Kevin Baker describes the model in that book as a city reduced to 150 equations and 200 parameters, with no neighbourhoods, parks or roads in it.[^15] Baker's account is also a warning. Forrester's assumptions about the urban poor produced the model's conclusion that housing programmes harm cities, and Baker recounts that officials in the Nixon administration took up that conclusion, with the authority of a computer behind it.[^15]

The people who built participatory maps in the 2000s could imagine a SimCity of a real place and could not build one. The view this wiki takes, set out with its evidence under ["It is too hard to write"](/about/objections/), is that three things have changed since: the data now exists for every place, computing costs far less, and the labour of assembling a model from many parts can be shared with language models. In the terms Anselm Hook used when asking for this article, the field has moved from maps, to analysis and prediction of single layers such as fire or water, and is now at "a phase transition" where brute-force models of a whole system have become possible. That is a claim about what can now be attempted. The same page records that, in a reading of this wiki's entries on 10 November 2026, none described a running model of one named place with its water, its living things, its buildings and its people in one loop. [Learned models of the Earth](/articles/learned-models-of-the-earth/) describes the one layer, the atmosphere, where a learned model demonstrably works, and why that success does not yet carry over to a city or a watershed.

## What the history suggests

The points below are this article's own reading of the record above.

- **The specialists' tools did not go away, and neither did the specialists.** Web mapping added a second group of makers and a much larger group of readers. A model of a place is likely to follow the same course: hydrologists will go on using their own solvers, and the new work is in putting the results in front of people who will never open one. The [Communication](/communication/) and [Presentation](/presentation/) shelves collect that work.
- **A shared open base mattered more than any one application.** OpenStreetMap outlasted nearly every mash-up built in the same years. The equivalents for models are open data about places and open engines, which is what the [Data sources](/data-sources/) and [Engines](/engines/) shelves list.
- **Being able to take part is different from having a say.** Haklay's criticism applies with more force to a model than to a map, because a model carries assumptions that are harder to see, as Forrester's did. A model offered to the public should state its assumptions and be [checked against what happened](/guides/check-it-against-what-happened/).
- **Novelty carried the first wave and then ran out.** People were glad to have participatory maps at all. A participatory model will get the same welcome once, and after that it will be judged on whether it changed a decision. The article on [how people respond to what a model shows them](/articles/how-people-respond-to-what-a-model-shows/) covers what is known about that.
- **Pictures drawn in a room and scenarios told as stories are part of the same craft.** The facilitators and scenario planners worked without computation, on the problem a model also has to solve: getting a group to hold one account of a system and to compare several futures.
