The 2026 Living Planet Index report (the WWF Living Planet Report 2026) came out on 8 October 2026, the day the article was published. Its headline figure is an average 73% decline in wildlife populations between 1970 and 2022. Five vertebrate groups are counted: mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish. Invertebrates and plants are not in the index yet.

The figure does not say:

- how many species are gone
- how many populations or individuals have been lost
- what share of species or populations are shrinking
- how many extinctions there have been

Headlines that read it any of those ways are wrong. Each of 35,803 studied populations gets a rate of change, and the index is the weighted geometric mean of those rates (an average taken by multiplying values rather than adding them). Only a few populations have records back to 1970. Many have short or sparse series.

The 2022, 2024 and 2026 editions reported 69%, 73% and 73%. The shift between editions is not a recent drop in wildlife. The whole trend line moves each time as the project adds populations and species and changes what it includes. About 1,000 populations and 300 species were added since the last report, with a deliberate push to cover Africa. Since 2024 only native species are counted.

Underneath the average, half of the studied populations were declining, 43% were increasing and 7% were stable. Ritchie argues that knowing which populations are in trouble, and why, matters more for directing effort than treating the whole as one decline.
