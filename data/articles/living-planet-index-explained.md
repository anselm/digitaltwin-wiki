The 2026 Living Planet Index report (the WWF Living Planet Report 2026) came out on 8 October 2026, the day the article was published.[^1] Its headline figure is an average 73% decline in wildlife populations between 1970 and 2022.[^1] Five vertebrate groups are counted: mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish. Invertebrates and plants are not in the index yet.[^1]

The figure does not say:

- how many species are gone
- how many populations or individuals have been lost
- what share of species or populations are shrinking
- how many extinctions there have been[^1]

Headlines that read it any of those ways are wrong.[^1] Each of 35,803 studied populations gets a rate of change, and the index is the weighted geometric mean of those rates (an average taken by multiplying values rather than adding them).[^1] Only a few populations have records back to 1970. Many have short or sparse series.[^1]

The 2022, 2024 and 2026 editions reported 69%, 73% and 73%.[^1] The shift between editions is not a recent drop in wildlife. The whole trend line moves each time as the project adds populations and species and changes what it includes.[^1] About 1,000 populations and 300 species were added since the last report, with a deliberate push to cover Africa.[^1] Since 2024 only native species are counted.[^1]

Underneath the average, half of the studied populations were declining, 43% were increasing and 7% were stable.[^1] Ritchie argues that knowing which populations are in trouble, and why, matters more for directing effort than treating the whole as one decline.[^1]
