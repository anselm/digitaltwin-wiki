The question is what happens to world grain prices and regional diets when several major producing regions fail in the same year, and how much more likely that becomes under climate change.[^1] The authors are Kilian Kuhla, Maria Goldrina, Michael J Puma, Yann Kinkel and Christian Otto, who work at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, the University of Potsdam and Columbia University.[^1]

The method is a stress test. The authors build probabilistic scenarios of simultaneous production failures for wheat, maize and soybean, under historical (2006–2015) and +2 °C climate conditions.[^1] The scenarios force Agrimate, an agent-based model of agricultural trade (a kind of model that simulates many separate actors and the outcomes of their interactions).[^1]

Agrimate simulates trade between 28 macro-regions at bi-weekly resolution, tracking production, trade flows, and commercial and strategic stocks.[^1] Prices, storage levels and consumption adjust as a supply shock spreads through the trade network.[^1] Each run covers ten years, with a one-year shock and two years of response, and the study runs 10 000 realisations per crop and climate condition, 60 000 in all.[^1,2] The model is calibrated to the 2022 food price crisis and to 2019–2021 export patterns.[^1] Trade-policy responses such as export restrictions are left out on purpose.[^1]

Under +2 °C the chance of a bi-weekly price spike above the historical 90th percentile nearly doubles for wheat and grows 2.7 times for maize and 2.45 times for soybean.[^1] Regional consumption risk is very uneven: some regions see almost none, others more than 100 Mcal per person per year.[^1] Risk rises with concentrated production, import dependence and a small set of suppliers, and falls with higher stock-to-use ratios.[^1] When world demand surges, an unprotected exporting region can lose domestic supply.[^1] The authors call for diversified production and trade, better storage management and early-warning systems.[^1]

The paper is open access under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence.[^1] The data behind the findings are deposited on Zenodo (doi 10.5281/zenodo.20406423), with the version of the Agrimate model used for the study.[^1,2] Supplementary information holds the model description, and a full model description is cited to an earlier paper by Kuhla and colleagues.[^1]
