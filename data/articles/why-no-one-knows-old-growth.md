A 2024 report in Columbia Insight, a nonprofit environmental news site, on the US Forest Service's first nationwide inventory of mature and old-growth forest on federal land. An executive order from the Biden administration in April 2022 directed the inventory; the agency completed it in April 2023. It was meant to support a plan to leave older trees standing as carbon stores. The article's point is that the maps it produced show the forest but not the trees: they give one classification per "fireshed" of about 250,000 acres, so, as the conservation director of Oregon Wild puts it in the piece, they give a total picture of the volume but cannot identify any particular stand of old growth anywhere.

The inventory rests on the Forest Inventory and Analysis program, a long-running set of about 355,000 field plots across the country, each around two and a half acres and spaced roughly one per 6,000 acres of forest. A retired Forest Service scientist now at the Woodwell Climate Research Center says those plots are adequate for a state or a group of national forests but too sparse to characterise a typical old-growth stand. The Forest Service official whose team oversaw the inventory says the agency did not want to create the impression that it knew exactly where the clumps of old forest were, because being wrong about a specific small area could lead to disputes, and that fine-grained mapping should happen locally. The agency's own estimate is that old growth covers 17 percent of the federal land inventoried and mature forest 45 percent.

Put as a question a model of a forest would answer: where exactly is the old growth, stand by stand, rather than how much of it there is in a 390-square-mile block. The piece looks at one way the Forest Service says it is exploring to close the gap, GEDI, the NASA-led lidar instrument on the International Space Station whose project lead is a professor at the University of Maryland. It had been used to map old growth in Australia but not in this inventory, and at the time of writing it was being stowed to make room for a Department of Defense project, according to the Forest Service official. Whether and when the Forest Service would use it was unclear.

Wild Heritage, a project of the Earth Island Institute, reposted a summary of the article on 12 December 2024 under its mapping and mature-and-old-growth tags, repeating the point that the coarse maps cannot identify specific stands and that GEDI remained inactive.
