Robert Rosen was an American theoretical biologist, born in Brooklyn on 27 June 1934 and died on 28 December 1998.[^1] He took a PhD in relational biology under Nicolas Rashevsky at the University of Chicago in 1959, stayed there until 1964, and then moved to the University at Buffalo with a joint appointment at its Center for Theoretical Biology.[^1] In 1975 he went to Dalhousie University in Halifax, Nova Scotia, as a Killam Research Professor in physiology and biophysics, and stayed until he took early retirement in 1994.[^1] He was president of the Society for General Systems Research, now the International Society for the Systems Sciences, in 1980 and 1981.[^1]

His work asked what life is, and held that a reductionist physics of mechanisms could not answer the question.[^1] He proposed relational biology, which treats an organism by its organisation, the relations among its parts and with time and environment, and devised the (M,R) systems as minimal relational models of a living thing.[^1] A year-long sabbatical at the Center for the Study of Democratic Institutions in Santa Barbara led him to what he called anticipatory systems theory.[^1] His book Anticipatory Systems sets out what he called the modelling relation between a natural system and a formal one, and separates a true modelling relation from a simulation not based on such a relation.[^1] Its publisher credits the book with founding the area of anticipatory systems theory.[^2] His daughter, Judith Rosen, writes that she spends much of her time making his work accessible, and that she counts anticipatory systems theory as its most important part.[^3]

He is the author of [Anticipatory Systems](/media/anticipatory-systems/), and his definition of an anticipatory system appears in the entry on [foresight and anticipation](/terms/foresight-and-anticipation/). The [Handbook of Anticipation](/media/handbook-of-anticipation/) includes him in its historical overview of earlier thinkers.[^4]
