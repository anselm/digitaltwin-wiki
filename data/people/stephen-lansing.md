J. Stephen Lansing, born in 1950, is an American anthropologist and complexity scientist.[^2] He is an external professor at the Santa Fe Institute and the Complexity Science Hub Vienna and emeritus professor of anthropology at the University of Arizona; from 2015 to 2019 he was founding director of the Complexity Institute at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore.[^1] He took his doctorate in anthropology at the University of Michigan in 1977, taught at the University of Southern California until 1996, and joined the University of Arizona in 1998.[^2]

Most of his work is on Bali. With the ecologist [James Kremer](/people/james-kremer/) he modelled the water temple networks that set planting and irrigation schedules for the island's rice terraces, and showed that the networks can organise themselves.[^2] His research on the water temples was the basis for Bali's listing as a UNESCO World Heritage cultural landscape in 2012.[^1] He wrote about it in *Priests and Programmers* (1991) and *Perfect Order: Recognizing Complexity in Bali* (2006). His most recent book, with Murray Cox, is *Islands of Order: A Guide to Complexity Modeling for the Social Sciences* (Princeton, 2019).[^1,2,3] He is working with Balinese colleagues to reduce greenhouse gas emissions from rice paddies, and with the Leakey Foundation and the Nature Conservancy to help hunter-gatherers in Borneo.[^1]

He is co-author of the 1993 paper [Emergent Properties of Balinese Water Temple Networks](/articles/lansing-kremer-balinese-water-temple-networks/). The [Bali scenario](/scenarios/bali-subak-water-temples/) starts from that model, and the [questions people ask](/about/faq/) page uses it as its example of a model that showed officials what farmers already knew.
