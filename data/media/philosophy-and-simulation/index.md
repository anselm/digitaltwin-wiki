The subject is emergence, the appearance in a whole of properties that its parts do not have, and what computer simulation experiments add to the case that it is real.[^1] DeLanda states the aim as an "emergent materialist world view that finally does justice to the creative powers of matter and energy".[^2] The "synthetic reason" of the subtitle is, in Yuk Hui's summary, a reasoning that cannot be reduced to deduction and is better thought through computation and mathematical models.[^2]

The book has an introduction, eleven chapters and an appendix.[^3] Most chapter titles pair a kind of simulation with what it has been used to grow:

- Introduction: Emergence in history
- The storm in the computer
- Cellular automata and patterns of flow
- Artificial chemistries and the prebiotic soup
- Genetic algorithms and the prebiotic soup
- Genetic algorithms and ancient organisms
- Neural nets and insect intelligence
- Neural nets and mammalian memory
- Multiagents and primate strategies
- Multiagents and Stone Age economics
- Multiagents and primitive language
- Multiagents and archaic states
- Appendix: Links to assemblage theory

A cellular automaton is a grid of cells, each updated from the state of its neighbours, and a genetic algorithm breeds candidate solutions over many generations, keeping the fitter ones. A multiagent simulation is built from many individual actors, each following its own rules, the kind of model built in [Growing Artificial Societies](/media/growing-artificial-societies/). The appendix connects the chapters to assemblage theory, the subject of DeLanda's 2006 book *A New Philosophy of Society*.[^2]

In Andrew Goffey's account, DeLanda asks an explanation of an emergent whole to cover its properties, its tendencies, such as the ways a fluid can move, and its capacities, such as a knife's capacity to cut.[^1] Tendencies and capacities need never be exercised. DeLanda places them in a "possibility space", a mathematical structure that theoretical biology, Goffey adds, sometimes calls a fitness landscape.[^1] Convection shows similar patterns in chemical and meteorological processes, and sets of equations capture those patterns. DeLanda takes this as evidence that part of emergence is independent of mechanism and that a simulation reproducing the pattern points to something real.[^1]

Goffey calls the book "clearly written and well-documented".[^1] He objects that the argument rests on a match between the behaviour of mathematical models and the "available evidence" in each field, and that the book does not say what counts as evidence.[^1] It does not examine research critical of simulation, or how models are derived and used in particular sciences.[^1] His example is animal ecology, where under some conditions species evade the predator-prey dynamics that the models reproduce.[^1] Hui, in *Computational Culture*, objects that simulation in the book serves "solely as the justification of the hypothesis of the synthetic processes" and that simulation itself "hasn't really been addressed".[^2]

Continuum published the book in London and New York in 2011.[^1,2] Open Library's record of that edition gives 2010 and 240 pages; Goffey's review gives 226 pages.[^1,3] Bloomsbury editions followed in 2015 and 2019, the last in the Bloomsbury Revelations series.[^4,5] The author's name is printed DeLanda on the cover and written De Landa in Goffey's review.[^1]
