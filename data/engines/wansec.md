The University of the West Indies at Mona profiles the project.[^1] The question is whether thickets of elkhorn coral (Acropora palmata) can act as a natural coastal defence by taking energy out of waves and trapping sediment.[^1] The work pairs coral restoration with coastal risk modelling and adaptive management tools, backed by US$2 million.[^1]

In Antigua and Barbuda the National Parks Authority planted about 2,500 elkhorn colonies in Rendezvous Bay, inside Nelson's Dockyard National Park.[^2] They average one coral per square metre, the same high density used on all three islands.[^2] The aim is dense stands that restore reef function over time.[^2] Severe bleaching during the fourth global mass bleaching event killed much of the Antiguan nursery and parent stock.[^2] The project then shifted toward propagating colonies that survived the heat.[^2] It also trained community members in restoration and started doctoral research on the distribution and genetic diversity of elkhorn coral across the Eastern Caribbean.[^2]

The intended outputs are replicable restoration strategies, stakeholder training and policy guidance for coral-based coastal protection in Caribbean small island states.[^1]

The Antigua and Barbuda component was announced complete on 26 August 2026.[^2]
