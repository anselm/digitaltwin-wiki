A road, a runway, a lit car park or a fence changes a place for everyone who can hear, see or smell it, and for the animals who navigate by those things. Someone wants to know what a change would do, or why a place already feels wrong.

## What you are really asking

- **Where does the noise, the light or the smell reach**, and how loud, bright or strong is it there?
- **Who notices**: people trying to sleep, birds timing their song, frogs calling, insects navigating by the sky?
- **What would a barrier, a dimmer, a reroute or a quiet hour change?**

## What kind of model answers it

Noise and light propagate in ways that are well understood, and **propagation models** map them from sources across terrain and buildings. The harder half is the effect: what a given level does to a species or a neighbourhood, which comes from field studies rather than physics. Agent-based models of animal movement are used to ask what a barrier or a corridor does to where animals can go.

This site does not yet list an engine for this question. [A frog chorus](/stories/a-frog-chorus/) is a short story of what one such study found, and [Blue Corridors](/data-sources/blue-corridors-bluecorridors-org/) is a map of whale migration routes with the threats laid alongside, the ocean version of the same question.

## What you need

- The sources: where the road, the runway, the lights are, and their schedule.
- Terrain and buildings.
- Who and what is there: residents, and species records such as [iNaturalist](/data-sources/inaturalist/) observations.
- Measurements: a sound meter on a few nights is cheap and is the check on any model.

## Without a model

Go at night. Listen and look. Ask residents when it is worst. A map of what you heard, hour by hour, is the thing a model would produce.
