The Strait of Hormuz is the only sea route out of the Persian/Arabian Gulf. Portolan, citing the US Energy Information Administration for the first half of 2025, puts its normal traffic at about 20.9 million barrels a day of crude and refined products. That is about a fifth of the world's oil, and about a fifth of the world's liquefied natural gas passes the same way. Only two pipelines bypass it, Saudi Arabia's East-West line to the Red Sea and the UAE's line to Fujairah, and they carry a fraction of its volume. Nothing bypasses it for Qatar's gas. The United States and Israel began bombing Iran on 28 February 2026. Five of the insurance clubs that cover shipowners cancelled their war-risk cover in the Gulf and the Gulf of Oman from 5 March, and by late that day the tracking firm Windward counted no tankers in transit. On 11 March the members of the International Energy Agency agreed to make 400 million barrels from emergency reserves available.

Since then the strait has opened and closed several times under ceasefires, blockades and a June memorandum that expired in August. Since the June memorandum it has split into a northern route approved by Iran and a southern route through Omani waters backed by the United States. IMF PortWatch's satellite ship-tracking counted 3.7 ships a day in the week to 18 October, against annual averages of about 85 to 92 a day in 2023 to 2025. The UK's maritime security office, as gCaptain reported, counted 254 transits in the week to 14 October, about 70% below pre-war traffic. Only 42 of them were seen on satellite tracking; the rest were crossings the US reported facilitating, including ships with transponders off, which the office could not verify. In mid-July the International Maritime Organization reported 300 to 400 ships and some 6,000 seafarers still stranded in the Gulf.

## Who needs an answer

- Governments and energy agencies holding emergency oil reserves, deciding how fast to release them and for how long.
- Gulf producers choosing between the strait, the bypass pipelines and night runs to the Gulf of Oman.
- Ship operators and insurers pricing each crossing, and seafarers' organisations tracking crews still stuck.
- Importing countries planning fuel and fertiliser supply around a route that may reopen and close again.

## The domains it chains

Oil and gas production in the Gulf; the ships, crews and insurance that move it; pipeline capacity around the strait; reserves and prices in importing countries; and, further out, the food and materials that depend on fuel and on cargoes that pass the same way.

## What a twin should let people try

- Capacity: if the strait stays restricted, how much can the bypass pipelines and the Omani route carry, and for how long can reserves cover the gap?
- Counting: how far do satellite counts understate traffic when many ships sail dark, and what other evidence fills the gap?
- Reopening: when ships leave together, how long until ports, crews and cargo flows return to normal, and in what order?
