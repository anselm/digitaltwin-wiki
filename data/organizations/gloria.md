The plots are one metre square, laid out just below the summits. The same plots on 62 summits across 16 European mountain ranges have been resurveyed every seven years since 2001. That gives four surveys and 21 years of records from 896 plots.

A study published in Science in 2026, led by Johannes Wessely and Stefan Dullinger with GLORIA colleagues, draws on those records. Local extinctions of plant species on the summits have risen and are speeding up from one survey to the next. The trend shows in single plots, on summits and across whole ranges. Summit species counts have still gone up, because lowland species arrive faster than cold-adapted specialists disappear. Each extra 0.1 °C of warming over the preceding 14 years went with a measurable rise in the extinction rate. Warming explained the pattern better than time alone. Species were most likely to vanish from their warm, low-elevation margins, and a decline in cover was an early though noisy warning sign.

Jonas Lembrechts of The 3D lab, writing about the study in September 2026, suggests the losses may be the first instalment of an extinction debt: losses still to come from warming that has already happened.
