# Patrons: who the site is for, told as stories

Anselm, 2026-10-18, on the first draft of this (which lived for a few hours
as `meta/patrons.md`): thinking goes in dated devlog entries, never in a
`meta/` file, because such files are ungrounded and age into noise. So this
is the entry, and the `meta/` file is gone.

## The meta goals this serves

- **The site is actionable, not passive.** A guest arrives with a problem
  and should leave able to do something about it. The contrast Anselm
  draws is cyborganthropology.com: curatorial, informational, and inert.
- **The site is evergreen.** Not today's latest idea. The scanner and the
  inbox are fuel, not the product.
- **Therefore noise is culled hard.** Not because every entry must pass a
  stiff test, but because an actionable, evergreen site cannot carry
  eighty one-line names in a section a guest is expected to use.
- **Persona is the root.** Every decision about what is good enough, what
  is pinned, what gets a section, is asked as "which guest is this for, and
  what do they do next?"

## The bar: Wikipedia's, not a per-kind test

The earlier draft set a test per kind (an engine must say cost and who
uses it, a guide must explain every term). Anselm: too high a bar, and
people are not dumb. The bar is closer to Wikipedia's content policies,
which are three: neutral point of view, verifiability, and no original
research (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Core_content_policies), with
inclusion governed separately by notability, meaning the thing has been
covered by sources independent of itself.

For us that reads:

- **Factual and neutral.** No adjectives about quality, no marketing, no
  hedging about our own process. `meta/curation.md` already says this.
- **Sourced.** Every claim is on a page that was read and listed. The
  third patron below must be able to quote an entry.
- **Unauthored.** The house voice, not a byline. Many hands will edit.
- **Included for use.** An entry exists because a patron could use the
  thing or learn from it. Who it is for is carried by tags and domains,
  not by prose about audiences.
- **Drafts are fine.** We are sketching the site; a draft is a sketch, not
  a failure. Production hides drafts by the build's `SHOW` setting and the
  staging host shows them. That is the flag; no sign-in, no url parameter,
  until there is a reason for one.

## The patrons, as people

Anselm's ask: tell a story of how a person comes to the site, what they
click, what they read, how it helped, as real as we can imagine, and what
they really need separate from what we can deliver. Then that is the
benchmark. The first two are his; the rest fill out the spread. None of
these people exist, and the "today" lines are checked against `data/` as
it stands on 2026-10-18.

### Mary, a student, one neighbourhood

Mary is at Berkeley studying urban climate impacts. She wants to pick her
own neighbourhood and ask what more tree cover would do: heat islands,
summer electricity bills, stormwater, bird populations, pests, leaf
sweeping, whether the trees should bear fruit, and what it all costs the
city over twenty years. She has used QGIS in a class and has a laptop.

She searches "simulate tree cover heat neighbourhood" and lands on a
question page. She needs it to say, in a few hundred words: this is three
questions, not one. Benefits of trees (energy, stormwater, air, carbon)
have mature tools; microclimate and heat have research-grade tools that a
student can run; budget, pests, birds and leaf sweeping have no tool, and
the honest move is a spreadsheet beside the model, with the sources for
each number. Then: here are the two tools, here is the data (a tree
inventory, canopy from LiDAR, surface temperature from Landsat, census
blocks), here is a worked example someone published, and here is how
people have told this story with a map and a before/after.

What she needs that we cannot deliver: the city's own tree inventory and
budget. What we could deliver: the question page, the tool entries, the
data entries, one worked example, and a guide on telling the story.

Today she finds: friGIS (hosted, German, pricing unknown), a tree map of
New York, a question page "Weather and climate" with one sentence, and ten
guides that each say "Not yet written". The tools she actually needs are
not in the wiki. From memory, not from pages read this session, the
candidates are i-Tree (USDA Forest Service, tree benefits), ENVI-met and
PALM-4U (microclimate), and UMEP (a QGIS plugin for urban heat); each
needs its own page read before it gets an entry. **She leaves with
nothing.**

### Ben Amar, a city engineer, one policy

Ben came from Tunisia, works for a mid-sized city's engineering
department, and bikes to work. He wants to know whether a zoning change
near the centre would reduce car traffic, whether it would save lives, and
what it would cost in total, because a councillor asked him and he has two
weeks. He is technical but not a modeller, and he cannot buy software
without a procurement process.

He lands on a question page about land use and traffic. He needs it to
say: land use and travel are coupled, and the coupled models are UrbanSim
and MATSim; street-level traffic and closures are SUMO; there is one open
tool, A/B Street, built for exactly the "what if we changed this street"
play he wants, in a browser. Safety and total cost have no commodity tool;
the practice is to take the traffic model's outputs into the published
crash-rate and cost methods, and the page should name those. Then: a city
that did this and published, so he can show the councillor; what the data
is (the street network from OpenStreetMap, counts the city already has,
the zoning layer); and what to ask a consultant if two weeks is not
enough.

Today he finds: a question "Housing and land use" with one sentence,
UrbanSim, MATSim and SUMO as survey drafts of two lines each, "After a
closure" as a question with no answer, no A/B Street, no twin of a city
that did it, no cost or safety method. **He finds three names and no
path.**

### Dolores, a mandate and no modeller

Dolores runs operations at a water district. After a flood the board told
her to "look into a digital twin", and a vendor has already called. She
has never run a model and does not intend to. She needs plain words for
what a twin is and is not, the kinds that exist, who has done one for a
district like hers and what happened, what the vendors on her desk
actually sell, what data she already owns, roughly what this costs and how
long it takes, and five questions to ask before signing.

She clicks "I have a mandate" on the home page, or she will if it exists.
She reads a guide on naming the question, then a question page on
flooding, then a twin entry for a place like hers, then two vendor entries,
then a short page on what to ask.

Today she finds: the flooding question (one sentence), Delft3D, ANUGA,
Delft-FEWS and Google Flood Hub as drafts, Virtual Singapore and
Helsinki/Bologna as proposed twins, Bentley iTwin, Esri and Xylem Vue as
drafts, and "Name the question" unwritten. **The bones are there; none of
it is written for her.** She is the best served of the three so far, and
she is the reason the site exists.

### Tomas, a builder with six weeks

Tomas is a backend developer at a small consultancy. A client wants a
prototype flood or traffic twin of their town and he has six weeks. He
needs an engine he can run tonight with real documentation, data he can
download without an account, a worked example repository he can copy, the
file formats everyone uses, and the one book that set the practice so he
is not reinventing it.

He clicks "I want to build one". He reads a guide on the smallest version,
opens an engine, opens the data it needs, clones an example.

Today he finds: Bay Layers with its source on GitHub (a real worked
example), seventy-nine engine names of which a dozen have a real body,
nineteen data sources, twenty books, and "Build the smallest version"
unwritten. **He is served by the example and the books, and let down by
the engines.**

### Dr. Okafor, keeping up

A hydrologist at a research institute who already builds models. She wants
what is new and credible each month, with sources she can cite, and she
would rather pull the wiki's exports into her own notes than read the
site. She clicks Recent, or fetches the machine-readable export.

Today she finds: Recent, posts, exports, and books. The inbox is not for
her. **She is served, as long as the entries she lands on are sourced.**

### Rosa, a reporter with a council vote

Rosa writes for a local paper. A consultant's model says a development
will not worsen traffic and the council votes next week. She needs to
understand what kind of model that is, what it cannot know, and three
questions to ask the consultant in public. She has an hour.

She searches a term from the consultant's report, lands on a term entry,
follows it to a guide on checking a model against what happened.

Today she finds: nine terms, and "Check it against what happened"
unwritten. **She gets a definition and no questions.**

### Mr. Reyes, a teacher with a classroom

A high-school geography teacher wants a forty-minute exercise where the
class plays with a real model of their region in a browser. He needs
things that run without installing anything.

Today he finds: Bay Layers, which orbits in the browser, and En-ROADS,
which runs in the browser. **He is served by two entries, and nothing
tells him they are the two.**

## The spread

The axes the seven cover: technical skill (none, some, coder), power to
act (student, officer, mandate-holder, reporter), scale (neighbourhood,
city, district, region, planet), time (an hour, two weeks, six weeks, a
term), and whether they want to act, build, judge, teach or keep up. A
new persona is worth adding when it sits off every existing one on at
least two axes. Vendors, completists and editors are real people who also
arrive, and are not catered to.

What the table says: the site is weakest exactly where it most wants to be
strong, the person with a specific question about their own place. The
cheapest wins are the question pages and the guides, which are prose, not
catalogue work. The engines section is the second problem and is catalogue
work.

## Home page

"Start here" is really the pinned entries, and Anselm's preferred word is
"Pinned". The heading now says that. The rule that pinned entries are
launching-off points, at least one for each patron who acts, builds,
judges or teaches, is editorial guidance held here, not enforced by code.
The home page carries many jobs, as home pages do, and a curator keeps the
pins honest by walking the stories above every so often.

## Repositories

How we got here: the scanner polls sources for the inbox, and GitHub is
one source among papers, news and podcasts. The repo feeds are fuel for
finding engines, never entries in themselves, and they sit in Sites, out
of the nav. Anselm's sharper point stands on its own: there is no
Ableton or Photoshop for a bioregion, no commodity tool that lets a person
pull up their own place and play with outcomes. The site should say so
plainly, because that is what Mary and Ben most need to hear first, and
then lay out the pieces that exist and how people join them. That is a
guide, and it may be the most useful page on the site.

## Papers

Anselm likes seeing new thinking (the recent Google work impressed him)
and does not know the bar. A proposal: a paper gets an entry when it gives
a patron something to use (a model, a dataset, code, a method with a
worked case) or when it is a landmark that later work builds on. A result
on its own stays in the inbox and may surface in Recent. Books are the
model: real, durable, and the section is a good start.
