# The stack: what a running twin is made of

Anselm, 2026-10-19, happy with the table of contents, thinking about the
Technologies area: a running "digital twin" is many pieces, and the site
could illuminate the parts better. Casually, the stack is: raw data; how
that data is structured or understood, a conversion from raw data to
knowledge; simulations that change values over time, snow cover growing,
a fire moving across forested mountains; and presentation. In rough
order: data, ontologies, engines that grind outcomes forward in time,
presentation layers.

## The observation

Technologies had two shelves, Engines and Data, and the engines shelf
carried everything that was not data: scene formats, 3D-city vendors,
visualisation tools, world models, standards, platforms. The task groups
help a guest find a flood model, but they hide the shape of the thing a
guest is going to build. Every twin on the site has the same four parts,
and the parts are made by different people, bought separately, and fail
separately:

1. **Data.** What was measured: observations, surveys, records, rasters,
   sensor feeds.
2. **Ontologies.** How the data is cut into things and relations so that a
   model, or a person, can reason about it: schemas, taxonomies, data
   models, standards, vocabularies. The step from data to knowledge.
3. **Engines.** The simulations that move state forward in time.
4. **Presentation.** Globes, maps, dashboards, stories: how a person sees
   what the model did.

The diagram in Anselm's post "What If? Time Machines" (anselm.substack.com,
2026-09-24) paints the complexity of a whole-system, macro-scale twin: six
families of observation feeding one engine, many independent models
beside it, a test-against-the-world loop, branches of futures, a layer
that makes them understandable, the people affected, and an act-observe-
learn loop back to the top. It names Ryan Orbuch's proposed world model as
the engine, which is one proposal among many; the shape around it is
general, so the diagram is recruited with its caption saying so.

## Ontologies have had no attention

Anselm's point, in his words as closely as I can keep them: reality is a
single seamless whole. Humans, and now machines, partition it into
discrete objects in order to comprehend it and to make rough models that
more or less correlate with real outcomes. A "thing" is a cluster of
tightly entangled relationships, almost always geographically bound but
not always: a dog is a thing and a government is a thing, denoted because
the denotation is useful, because a thing is roughly disjoint from other
things. John Muir: pick out anything by itself and you find it hitched to
everything else in the universe. Humans are very good at recognising these
clusters and labelling them. For how categories actually work he leans on
prototype theory (Eleanor Rosch) and George Lakoff.

Two things follow for the site. First, every engine on the site embodies
an ontology, usually an unstated one: a hydraulic model's world is cells
and fluxes; an agent-based model's world is agents and rules; a city
model's world is buildings with heights and uses. Coupling two engines is
mostly the work of reconciling two ontologies. Second, the ontology layer
is where the field is thinnest and where the papers are, so the shelf
should hold papers and ideas as well as standards and tools.

Citations gathered today, all read:

- Donges, J. F., Lucht, W., Cornell, S. E., Heitzig, J., Barfuss, W.,
  Lade, S. J., Schlüter, M. (2021). Taxonomies for structuring models for
  World–Earth systems analysis of the Anthropocene. Earth System Dynamics
  12, 1115–1137. doi:10.5194/esd-12-1115-2021. Three taxa for modelled
  subsystems (biophysical, socio-cultural, socio-metabolic) and a scheme
  for classifying the interactions between them, so that components from
  different modelling traditions can be selected and combined.
- Prototype theory: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype_theory (Rosch, 1971
  onward; graded membership; a robin is a better bird than a penguin).
- Lakoff, Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things (1987):
  en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women,_Fire,_and_Dangerous_Things (radial
  categories, idealised cognitive models, fuzzy boundaries).
- Muir, My First Summer in the Sierra (1911), via en.wikiquote.org/wiki/John_Muir.
- Talisman, J. (2026). Ontology Pipeline: A Framework for Knowledge
  Engineering. Technics Publications, ISBN 9798898161392; site
  ontologypipeline.com with a browser Studio and a SKOS editor. Anselm
  sent a Modern Data 101 post about it first (2026-05-04) and then the
  site; what he wanted cited was the book. The entry is a book kind on the
  Ontologies shelf, linked liberally, written as an assessment of what the
  method offers a builder of a twin of a place; the book itself was not
  read, so the review rests on the publisher's contents list, the book
  page, the Studio and the three posts. The SKOS editor has its own entry.
- ontology.monster: Anselm's own experiment, a machine that reads the news
  and lets kinds and parts emerge from the text rather than from a fixed
  schema; repo anselm/ontology-scraper (public), README read.

## What changed on the site

- Technologies reads as the stack, in order: Data, Ontologies, Engines,
  Presentation. Data moved from last to first; two sections are new.
- **Ontologies** (`data/ontologies/`): the Donges et al. paper as an
  article entry, CityJSON (an OGC standard, an encoding of the CityGML
  data model) as a worked example of an ontology in daily use, and
  ontology.monster as a tool. The section intro carries the
  partition-of-a-seamless-whole idea in the house voice, with the
  citations above; a term entry, "Ontology", carries the longer form.
- **Presentation** (`data/presentation/`): ParaView moved from Engines;
  CesiumJS, MapLibre, deck.gl and kepler.gl written from their own pages
  with licences and open blocks.
- An article, "The parts of a digital twin" (`data/articles/parts-of-a-twin/`),
  walks the four layers and carries the recruited diagram with its credit
  and the caveat about the engine it names.
- "What is a digital twin?" links the parts article; Data and Engines
  intros say where they sit in the stack.
