## The register of the objections page

On 13 November Anselm asked whether formal evaluation such as a
Flesch-Kincaid grade would help. A rough count gave the three versions of the
objections page nearly the same grade: 9.1 on 10 November, 9.4 as written to
the rules on 11 November, 9.2 after the 12 November rewrite. Reading level
does not separate the version he disliked from the others.

He then asked for one paragraph at a college-graduate register. The
paragraph was the one on externalities in the "Esri does this, but it is
secret" answer. The version he was shown:

> If such proprietary models do exist, the objection restates the problem it
> was meant to dismiss. A firm or an agency that can simulate the
> consequences of its own decisions holds an informational advantage over the
> communities that will absorb those consequences, and that asymmetry is what
> allows costs to be externalised: a harm that only the decision-maker can
> foresee is one that nobody else is in a position to price, contest or
> litigate. An open model of the same region redistributes that foresight.
> Civic modelling is for that reason unavoidably political, since it alters
> the bargaining position of every party to the decision. The objection also
> carries an implicit concession, because a model worth concealing is, by the
> objector's own account, a model worth having.

What he said:

> I do prefer the second - it is more precise. For example 'secret' has a
> certain appeal, but proprietary, or proprietary and confidential, is more
> accurate. It is more verbose. Perhaps however this is one of my
> frustrations with the text; it elides or glosses over ideas in what feels
> to me to be a bit lazy - or sloppy.

## Data justice

Anselm sent a screenshot of a short video by the account c_devane
summarising an article, and said the topic is important. The article, from
the screenshot and from its Crossref record:

- Lina Dencik, Arne Hintz, Joanna Redden and Emiliano Treré, "Exploring Data
  Justice: Conceptions, Applications and Directions", Information,
  Communication & Society 22(7), 873-881, published online 13 May 2019,
  https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2019.1606268. It is the introduction to
  the journal's special issue on data justice, and free to read.

A commenter on the video recommends Mark Monmonier's book How to Lie with
Maps, on reading maps critically and on who makes them.

Neither has been opened or read for the wiki. No entry on the site mentions
data justice, Dencik or Monmonier. The nearest existing material is the
trust objection on the objections page (the CARE and OCAP principles) and
the guide Protect people's privacy. Where it belongs is Anselm's call: a
source for those two pages, a term, or entries on the Articles and Media
shelves. His rule of 4 November keeps the wiki to software, tools and data,
so the case for it rests on data governance and not on social theory.
