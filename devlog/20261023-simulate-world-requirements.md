# simulate.world read as requirements for the engine (2026-10-23)

Anselm pointed at his 2015 essay, https://simulate.world/ ("this older
essay also has some scenarios, goals an opinions"), the same day the
Scenarios shelf became the benchmark for the twin engine he plans to
build. The essay stays out of the wiki's public copy (house rule); this
entry pulls out what it asks of a tool, so the benchmark can be checked
against his own stated goals and not only against the scenarios.

## The goal, in his words

"The goal is to provide people and communities with tools that allow
them to simulate and predict the impact of new laws and policy on their
regional social, economic and environmental landscape." The models
exist already, but for "an elite"; the gap is "civic digital models
that ordinary people can inspect, use and challenge." And not to decide
for anyone: "If a community ratifies the cutting down of their own
forests - so be it. The goal is to just empower people to make more
complicated decisions together more effectively."

## Requirements a twin can be scored on

From chapter 07 (data, interface, benefits), restated as tests:

1. Open and forkable: "Anybody should be able to use or modify the
   models"; "many kinds of models ... written by different people",
   no single solution.
2. Contributable, curated: facts and relationships go into "some kind
   of shared open database", "similar to wikipedia ... curated to avoid
   spam". Relationships matter as much as data, and are documented
   worse.
3. Pre-populated but overridable: seeded data for novices, and
   stakeholders can "populate a scenario with their own data".
4. Calibrated: "calibrated against historical data and tested on
   observations not used to fit them"; early accuracy can be coarse,
   enough to "reject the most egregiously bad decisions".
5. Assumptions visible: stakeholders "question the inputs,
   relationships and model structure, compare outputs with
   observations, and see where uncertainty remains"; the weights on
   outcomes "must remain open to debate".
6. Simple at the surface: meet people "at their own level", including
   "cartoonifying" where needed, with different front ends for
   different audiences.
7. Runs forward in time on the facts and relationships, ideally
   distributed on people's own machines "to be more civic".
8. Variable resolution: "All farms ... may only produce one output; or
   ... broken down into specific kinds of crops".
9. Water first: "Water ... may need to be modeled especially
   accurately"; aquifer compaction, lagged drought, snowmelt, water
   rights, fish passage.
10. Tradeoffs shown, not settled: social, environmental and economic
    outcomes side by side; "deciding how to value those outcomes remains
    a community decision".
11. A focal object for debate: "like introducing a ball into a game";
    discussion aims at the model, not at each other.

Item 4 is the one his later point about scale sharpens: local
stakeholders are the people who can say whether a run matches what
happened.

## Scenarios taken from chapter 08

Lansing and Kremer's Bali water temples (with the 1993 paper as an
article), Yellowstone wolves, Alwar's johads, Grand Coulee and the
upper Columbia salmon, Lake Merritt, the Delta and its smelt, and the
San Joaquin Valley's groundwater (the essay's California drought,
narrowed to where the decision now sits under SGMA). Deepwater Horizon
was already on the shelf. Not taken: the Bali Fish Bank (one recorded
talk, nothing primary to write from yet) and Nestlé at Cascade Locks
(a 2015 proposal; what became of it was not checked, and whether a
settled past dispute belongs on the shelf is Anselm's call).

## Chapter 06's feared trajectory

The essay lists a feared sequence (manufactured doubt, failed
collective decisions, abandoned cities, food insecurity, war) and says
plainly it is "a feared scenario, not an inevitable sequence or a
calibrated forecast". It is a motive, not a scenario, and is not on
the shelf.
