What was found:

- COBALT's Bioregional Digital Twin (Gulf of Maine, Casco Bay, River Tay),

  funder's record of the proposal exists. It shares a name with Anselm's own
  terratwin work and is unrelated.

What was done: new draft entries twins/cobalt-bioregional-digital-twin and
twins/eu-terratwin, both tier 4 and marked in development; BioDT extended
with two paragraphs from the papers. Scores for pace, role and area on the
two new entries are a first pass like the rest.

Not done: Digital Gaia has no entry, since the page said to describe it is

## UrbanFootprint, read closely (2026-11-18)

Howard Silverman brought https://urbanfootprint.com/ to Anselm, who wrote:
"It seems to focus on resilience, not merely just simulate something. It
seems to have some multi-domain or more whole-systems emphasis. It does
appear to be proprietary. I would read this closely and evaluate - and try
decide where and how we should reference it if it feels useful."

Read: the front page, About, leadership, the three data foundation pages,
Analyst, Explorer, Direct Data, the four solutions pages, the community
resilience essay, the case studies for Duquesne Light, Envision Utah and
Louisiana DCFS, the October 2025 parcel-data post, the Analysis Modules page
of the product documentation, LightBox's press release, and the README of
the open version 1.5 on GitHub. Not read: the other case studies, the blog
beyond its first index page, the rest of the documentation, the SCAG
technical summary a search turned up. The product was not used.

What it turned out to be. The wiki already had it, sharing one entry with
Climate X, an unrelated British company, since the October import. It is
not a simulator. It is a parcel table for the whole United States with
land use, people and hazard layers aligned to it, composite scoring on top
(Duquesne Light ranks substations by the product of infrastructure,
hazard, exposure and vulnerability criteria), and a set of sketch-planning
modules that apply rates to a drawn land-use map. So Anselm's two
impressions hold in a particular sense: it is multi-domain because the
layers share one canvas, not because processes are coupled, and its
resilience is a ranking of where to spend, not a model of recovery. Its
own essay names social connectedness as a leading trait of a resilient
community, and its data pages list nothing that measures that.

Facts that matter for a reader: LightBox, a commercial real-estate data
firm, bought it on 24 June 2025; the newest blog post is October 2025 and
the newest item on the news page is from 2022; no prices; United States
only; the Conservation module is California only with its method on
request. The open ancestor, UrbanFootprint 1.5 (GPL-3.0, Calthorpe
Analytics), last changed in June 2018.

What was done. The shared entry was split, with no redirect, into
engines/climate-x and engines/urbanfootprint. The new entry sits in Land
use and housing beside UrbanSim, answers the housing and land use
question, and says in a section marked as the wiki's reading that the
modules have a state and no clock, linking to Inside an engine. It closes
with what a reader who cannot buy it can take: the parcel canvas, the
composite score (with the free FEMA National Risk Index as the open
equivalent), and the Envision Utah sequence of five drawn futures, the
same measures for each, and a survey answered by 10,000 people.

Not done, and worth considering: the Envision Utah project as a story or
a scenario of its own, from Envision Utah's pages and not only the
vendor's; a term entry for sketch planning; entries for Peter Calthorpe
and for the open version 1.5 if anyone gets it running; the composite
score as a guide ("rank before you simulate").

## CliMA's Earth system model (2026-11-19)

Anselm sent the coda of CliMA's series on the day it was published and
asked how it fits:
https://clima.caltech.edu/2026/11/19/coda-what-it-took-to-build-a-new-earth-system-model/

Read, through the summarising fetcher because the site answered a direct
download with "429 Too Many Requests": the coda, the opening post of 28
July, the coupler post of 5 November and the atmosphere post of 29
October. Also the GitHub organisation's repository list by API, and MIT's
2018 announcement. Not read: the other nine posts of the thirteen, any of
the linked papers (Yatunin and others 2026 on GPU throughput is the one
that would give measured speed), and the documentation. Nothing was run.

How it fits. It is a global model, so it is not a tool for a place, and
the entry says so in its first paragraph. It earns an entry (engines/clima,
draft, tier 2, Weather and climate, beside CESM and E3SM) for three
reasons: it is the clearest public worked example of the layers that
Inside an engine describes (solvers, a coupler exchanging conserved
quantities, calibration with parameters in one place, learned closures
inside a physical frame); its coda argues the physics-or-learning question
that divides the wiki's other climate entries, and argues it from the
need to answer counterfactuals, which is the wiki's own concern; and
parts of it (Oceananigans with ClimaOcean, ClimaLand,
EnsembleKalmanProcesses) are open and usable alone at regional scale.

What the entry is careful about: the posts give no skill scores and no
comparison with other models, the coupled model is not yet calibrated and
drifts at coarse resolution, and the forecasting cost advantage is an
estimate. Those are listed under "What has not been shown".

The picture the depiction tool fetched from CliMA's site was a 192-pixel
photograph of a storm from orbit, which shows nothing of the model, so
the entry has no picture. A figure from one of the posts would be the
right one if its licence allows.

Leads: an entry for Oceananigans by itself, since it is the piece a
coastal or estuary modeller would pick up; EnsembleKalmanProcesses and
CalibrateEmulateSample as calibration tools for any expensive model; the
Terence Tao essay of 23 September 2026 on trust in AI for science, which
the coda links; Tapio Schneider as a person entry; the ECMWF-led
perspective (arXiv 2606.25076) calling for modular, tested, GPU-ready
model codes.

## Fire risk to US structures, and a wish for a watch list (2026-11-19)

Anselm sent https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78904-z with the
words "I'm seeing things that I want to build models of and track in
general".

The paper (Iglesias and others, Nature Communications, 3 November 2026,
open access) was read in full except for part of the methods and all of
the supplement. Written as articles/fire-risk-to-us-structures-2060
(draft, tier 2), answering the wildfire question. The entry gives the
construction (area burned times number of structures times past loss per
area burned, each projected or held), the findings, the assumptions, and
a four-step version for one county. It ties to the UrbanFootprint and
FEMA National Risk Index entries as the same multiplication with time
added.

Three things found while reading, all stated in the entry: the text's
burned-area totals (589 million hectares from 1985 to 2023; up to 1,059
million in the West to 2060) exceed the land area of the contiguous
United States; the West's projected loss range has a lower bound above
the national one; and the Los Angeles comparison (16,000 structures as 7
to 23 per cent of the national total) does not follow from the stated
range. The code repository the paper cites returned 404 on 19 November
and is not among the lead author's public repositories.

On the wider remark. My reading, not his words: he wants the wiki, or
something beside it, to hold a standing list of subjects worth modelling
and worth watching, of which this is one. Vitals already tracks nine
global series; nothing tracks regional ones, and nothing pairs a tracked
series with "the simplest model you could build of this". The entry's
last section does that pairing for fire. A todo item under his name
records the wish and two shapes it could take. Nothing was built for it.

Leads: HISDAC-US, MTBS and ICS-209-PLUS have no data-source entries and
each deserves one; the Joseph and others wildfire model the paper relies
on; Balch and others 2024 on fast fires.

## The Grammar of Simulation (2026-11-21)

Anselm sent https://simgrammar.com/ as "fun": by looking at many
simulations it finds a common grammar among them. Read: the front page,
the README and LICENSE of the repository, and of the paper (version 0.8,
4 November 2026, about 500 kB of markdown) the abstract and sections 2.1,
2.5, 2.6, 4.1 and the start of 6. Not read: the family-by-family
sections, the technique landscape, the opportunities, the book, the
videos.

Written as articles/the-grammar-of-simulation (draft, tier 2). It is
about games, and the entry says so first. Its use here: its substrate
list (stocks, agents, grid fields, networks, continuous bodies) is nearly
the list in Inside an engine, made separately, and its layers for
control, legibility and pacing are what the wiki's engine entries mostly
lack. Inside an engine now has one sentence pointing to it. The entry
reports the authors' own limits: one model rated nearly everything, a
second model agreed poorly on some scales, no second person has coded.

Worth noting for this wiki's own practice, and not acted on: their build
fills every number in the prose from data and fails when an assertion
attached to a sentence stops holding. This wiki's entries state numbers
by hand.

Leads: the legibility mechanics (overlays, causal tooltips, agent
inspection, history chronicle) as a guide for the Communication shelf;
the "why did that happen" query over an event log as a pattern for public
models; SimCity's history as a planning tool already has mentions here.

## Anselm's own guide: reference it, do not copy it (2026-11-21)

Anselm wrote a guide in the esim room, "How to write your own digital
twin" (https://esimv1.exe.xyz/docs/guides/writing-a-digital-twin,
revision 1, dated today), and asked whether the wiki should copy it or
reference it. The Markdown file was read in full.

Decision made here, his to reverse: reference. Reasons. A copy would be a
second text to keep in step with a page that is at revision 1 and will
change. The guide already follows the order of the wiki's nine guides and
links to them, so a copy would put two versions of the same advice on one
site. It says of itself that it cites no source and is experienced
opinion, which is a different kind of page from a wiki entry. And until
now his own work has stayed off the wiki unless cited
(`devlog/20261024-stretching-and-tearing.md`); an entry that describes
the page and says plainly who made it keeps that line visible.

Done: articles/how-to-write-your-own-digital-twin (draft, tier 2, no
picture), which states that the author maintains this wiki, and one
sentence at the end of the Guides index pointing to it. The index is a
public page of human origin; the sentence is mine and waits on his read.

One risk in referencing: the address has a version in its host name
(esimv1.exe.xyz). If the documentation moves, the entry's link breaks.

What the guide has that the nine guides and the two engine articles do
not, found by searching them for the words. These are leads for the
wiki's own guides, not acted on:

- simulator against replay, with the three tests (no mention here);
- running faster than the event against slower for planning (the
  Scenarios shelf has a pace field; no guide explains it);
- the manifest, seeded randomness, accounts that must close, regression
  runs (no mention in the guides or in Inside an engine);
- several bottom lines not folded into an index, and stretching against
  tearing (a devlog entry of 24 October; nothing public);
- a section of instructions addressed to a language model. The wiki says
  it is readable by people's models and has no such page.
