# Bioregional and biodiversity twins sent by a correspondent

16 November 2026. Claude (Fable 5.2), room twin-compendium, session
https://claude.ai/code/session_018TGCFhFpKfqJ5fkxug9aM4.

Anselm passed on seven links a correspondent had gathered with Claude, and
asked: "Are these in our database? Are any of these actually predictive
models?" and then "None are predictive are they?" He said "Feel free to add."

What was found:

- COBALT's Bioregional Digital Twin (Gulf of Maine, Casco Bay, River Tay),
  described on a Buckminster Fuller Institute page. Not in the wiki. The page
  describes a data collection, a 3D interface and a story map. It names no
  simulation or forecast. Two of the four links for it were dead (bfi.org
  ?p=56863, said to cover Digital Gaia, and the Transformations Community
  workshop page); a third was the same page under another address.
- BioDT. Already an entry. The two papers were not among its sources. Both
  were read in full. The 2024 overview says four of the ten prototypes are
  mechanistic, that absolute predictions are impossible and the models rank
  scenarios, and that the hoped-for hybrid models would not be achieved.
- EU TerraTwin (CORDIS 101214557), 2026 to 2028. Not in the wiki. Only the
  funder's record of the proposal exists. It shares a name with Anselm's own
  terratwin work and is unrelated.

What was done: new draft entries twins/cobalt-bioregional-digital-twin and
twins/eu-terratwin, both tier 4 and marked in development; BioDT extended
with two paragraphs from the papers. Scores for pace, role and area on the
two new entries are a first pass like the rest.

Not done: Digital Gaia has no entry, since the page said to describe it is
gone and nothing else about it was read. The COBALT story map could not be
opened by a script and was not looked at.

Also on this day: the Santa Fe working group entry gained the event page's
opening line on "grand challenges", which Anselm pointed to. The event, the
SFI story and Karen Willcox were already in the wiki. He asked that Clement
Bezold's Anticipatory Democracy be tracked; it has been an entry since
2 November (media/anticipatory-democracy), with Bezold's own page.

## Later the same day: a history article on web mapping

Anselm raised the Bay Area mapping and design firms of the early 2000s
(Stamen, GreenInfo, The Grove, Global Business Network, the Institute for
the Future, Shaping San Francisco, XPLANE, from a search summary he pasted)
and asked whether that period is relevant now. He means to approach some of
those people, many of them friends, about funding a non-profit. Asked
whether to draft a history article, he said:

"sure go ahead and add that article - it's reasonable to have posts like
this i feel - helps contextualize a history of the movement. it's worth
noting that back in those days we were all fairly excited to have
participatory maps _at all_ - it was novel. i myself co-led the wherecamp
conferences for several years - it was a frothy and fun time. what has
happened more recently now is the possibility not just of maps or
visualizations or even some analysis and simple prediction of single layers
(fire, hydrology) but just now a possibility of brute force whole system
models to predict future outcomes - the vision that simcity had ... we
imagined such things were possible in the past, but now there is a phase
transition to them being actually possible."

Written: articles/what-web-mapping-did-to-gis (draft, tier 3). It names him
as a WhereCamp co-founder from his own about page, quotes the first remark
above, and attributes the "phase transition" argument to him while keeping
the wiki's standing concession that no whole-place model is known to run.
Three papers are cited from abstract or record only and marked so. The
search summary's claims about XPLANE's Bay Area presence and about GBN and
causal loop diagrams were not used. No entries were made for the firms
themselves; Stamen, GreenInfo and scenario planning are the candidates.

## Also the same day: a search-engine conversation as fuel

Anselm pasted a conversation with Google's AI mode ("here is a conversation
with google that may have some useful fuel"). He had asked it for local
issues a twin could model, for whole-system cases with knock-on effects and
trade-offs between stakeholders, for tools that join agent-based models to
physics, how much work a model takes, and whether any of the tools serve
civic outreach or citizen participation.

Checked against the wiki. Already present: GAMA, AnyLogic, NetLogo,
UrbanSim, MATSim, SUMO, FMI (under HELICS), synthetic populations.
Missing and now written as drafts, each from the maker's own pages:
communication/mit-cityscope, engines/palm, engines/swat, engines/riverware.
The GAMA entry gained the Hanoi HoanKiemAir project and NetLogo gained
HubNet.

What was not used, and why:

- The effort figures (70 to 80 percent of the work in data preparation; two
  to four weeks for a toy; six to eighteen months for a calibrated twin)
  came with no source that states them. They read as plausible and are not
  evidence. A sourced account of effort would be worth having.
- "HubNet Web: citizens log in on their phones". The NetLogo manual says
  HubNet is desktop clients only for now. HubNet Web exists as a separate
  site; only its front page was read.
- "Pareto frontier" as what such models produce: no source.

Leads not followed:

- Adade and de Vries, "A systematic review of digital twins' potential for
  citizen participation and influence in land use agenda-setting", Discover
  Sustainability 2025, doi 10.1007/s43621-025-01204-x. Springer refused the
  script. From the search summary only: 34 articles, citizens take part by
  supplying data or by using the twin, influence at agenda-setting is
  limited. This bears directly on his last question and should be read.
- Luo and others 2025, "Urban digital twins for citizen-centric planning"
  (84 publications); "Local Digital Twins for Cities and Regions: The Way
  Forward" (Springer 2025).
- RiverWISE and the RiverWare Viewer, as a public face of a licensed model.

The problem classes it listed, set against the Scenarios shelf. The shelf is
real cases Anselm sends, so none were written as entries; these are the
classes with no case yet: upzoning and infrastructure strain; congestion
pricing and traffic displaced to the edge of the zone (HoanKiemAir is a
worked example); seawall or managed retreat; a new park raising rents
around it; electrification and winter peak load; prescribed burning against
smoke; urban trees against water in dry cities; solar siting against
habitat. The watershed class (farms, salmon, hydropower) is already well
covered.

## Also the same day: GAMA, and a page on the parts of an engine

Anselm on GAMA: "a fairly generic agent platform - and very similar to what

He then wrote: "reinforcement learning sounds interesting - i didn't
appreciate this is a technique - or that digital twin engines would be
composed of a variety of techniques - drilling down into the stereotypical
parts of digital twin engines seems useful- we could use a page with a
diagram; for example there are cellular automata, and agent based models,
presumably other componenets".

Written as articles/inside-an-engine (draft, tier 3) with engine.svg, drawn
by script in the style of the wiki's other diagrams. It sits under
articles/parts-of-a-twin, which gives the four layers; this page opens the
engine layer. The division (state and clock; six kinds of process; how an
agent decides; coupling, assimilation, calibration, scenarios, search) is
the wiki's own and the page says so. Only three outside sources were read
for it; the rest rests on linked entries. A methods textbook should be
checked against it before it is promoted. The wiki has no engine entry that
is plainly a cellular automaton (a fire-spread or urban-growth one would
fill that), and no term entries for the individual techniques.

## Open Systems Foundation, looked at and not used (2026-11-18)

Anselm asked whether anything at https://opensystems.foundation/ was worth
plucking out. Read: the front page, About, Projects, Gaia Commons, GaiaOps,
GaiaOS, OPAL, PASEOs and Resources, and the list of repositories under
github.com/OSFadmin. Not read: the two PDFs (manifesto, culture), the
strategic drivers page, and gaiacommons.net, which is a script-only page
that returned no text to a plain fetch.

It is a charitable foundation in Vaduz, Liechtenstein, whose subject is how
regenerative groups organise: a coordination platform (GaiaOps, "in live
build"), a licence under development (OPAL, after the Peer Production
License), and a tiered scheme for organisations joining a commons (PASEO).
Nothing on the pages read is a model, a data source, or a tool for
describing a place. The nearest points are statements of intent: GaiaOS
lists "ecological indicators, sensors, and bioregional data" as a future
domain and names an open call to map existing projects, with no date and
no published component. The three GitHub repositories are the websites.

No entry written. Two things to keep in mind: OPAL, if it is published,
would belong beside the licences the wiki already discusses for data and
models; and the GaiaOS open call, if it opens, is a place a twin project
could be put forward. Planetir and Project Weave, named as partners, were
not followed.

## UrbanFootprint, read closely (2026-11-18)

Howard Silverman brought https://urbanfootprint.com/ to Anselm, who wrote:
"It seems to focus on resilience, not merely just simulate something. It
seems to have some multi-domain or more whole-systems emphasis. It does
appear to be proprietary. I would read this closely and evaluate - and try
decide where and how we should reference it if it feels useful."

Read: the front page, About, leadership, the three data foundation pages,
Analyst, Explorer, Direct Data, the four solutions pages, the community
resilience essay, the case studies for Duquesne Light, Envision Utah and
Louisiana DCFS, the October 2025 parcel-data post, the Analysis Modules page
of the product documentation, LightBox's press release, and the README of
the open version 1.5 on GitHub. Not read: the other case studies, the blog
beyond its first index page, the rest of the documentation, the SCAG
technical summary a search turned up. The product was not used.

What it turned out to be. The wiki already had it, sharing one entry with
Climate X, an unrelated British company, since the October import. It is
not a simulator. It is a parcel table for the whole United States with
land use, people and hazard layers aligned to it, composite scoring on top
(Duquesne Light ranks substations by the product of infrastructure,
hazard, exposure and vulnerability criteria), and a set of sketch-planning
modules that apply rates to a drawn land-use map. So Anselm's two
impressions hold in a particular sense: it is multi-domain because the
layers share one canvas, not because processes are coupled, and its
resilience is a ranking of where to spend, not a model of recovery. Its
own essay names social connectedness as a leading trait of a resilient
community, and its data pages list nothing that measures that.

Facts that matter for a reader: LightBox, a commercial real-estate data
firm, bought it on 24 June 2025; the newest blog post is October 2025 and
the newest item on the news page is from 2022; no prices; United States
only; the Conservation module is California only with its method on
request. The open ancestor, UrbanFootprint 1.5 (GPL-3.0, Calthorpe
Analytics), last changed in June 2018.

What was done. The shared entry was split, with no redirect, into
engines/climate-x and engines/urbanfootprint. The new entry sits in Land
use and housing beside UrbanSim, answers the housing and land use
question, and says in a section marked as the wiki's reading that the
modules have a state and no clock, linking to Inside an engine. It closes
with what a reader who cannot buy it can take: the parcel canvas, the
composite score (with the free FEMA National Risk Index as the open
equivalent), and the Envision Utah sequence of five drawn futures, the
same measures for each, and a survey answered by 10,000 people.

Not done, and worth considering: the Envision Utah project as a story or
a scenario of its own, from Envision Utah's pages and not only the
vendor's; a term entry for sketch planning; entries for Peter Calthorpe
and for the open version 1.5 if anyone gets it running; the composite
score as a guide ("rank before you simulate").

## PSI, looked at and not used (2026-11-18)

Steve Coy, who is not yet a formal contributor, sent https://psi.inc/.

## SeaSketch (2026-11-18)

A third link from Howard Silverman: https://www.seasketch.org/. Read: the
front page (rendered in a headless browser, since it is a script-only
page), five pages of the documentation (the introduction, whether it is
free, sketch classes, surveys, offline support), the READMEs of
seasketch/next and seasketch/geoprocessing with their dates and licences
from the GitHub API, and a UC Santa Barbara news article of 7 March 2025.
Not read: the case study pages, the geoprocessing documentation site, the
terms of use, and any paper about it. The software was not used.

Written as communication/seasketch (draft, group Playing), beside
Geodesignhub and answering the combining-proposals question. It is open
source (BSD 3-Clause), changed the day before the entry was written, and
free to use hosted for educational and non-profit work. It is the closest
thing found so far to what the wiki keeps describing in the abstract:
the people affected draw the proposals and each proposal comes back with
the same measures. Like UrbanFootprint its analysis is overlay with no
clock, and the entry says so as the wiki's reading. The closing section
suggests a simulation could sit behind a report in place of an overlay;
that is an inference, marked as one, and no project doing it was found.

Leads: Will McClintock as a person entry; MarineMap and the California
Marine Life Protection Act process as a story of participation with a
long record; the Maldives ocean use survey and the Azores network as
cases; a peer-reviewed account of SeaSketch in use, which this entry
lacks; Marxan, the reserve-selection tool often used with it, has no
entry here.
