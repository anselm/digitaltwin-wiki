# 2026-10-02 — audience: ordinary people with a real question

Point-in-time entry, fifth of the day. Written by Claude. Amends
`20261002-approach-sketch.md`; the corpus-and-modules structure stands, the
audience and the entry design change.

## Anselm's direction

He wants a real-world solutions resource, civic-minded, without the
priesthood of narrow expertise and jargon. The audience to design for: a
farmer, a city doing outreach, ordinary people who want to model a civic
issue. Specialists are welcome but are not who the site is written for.
Nobody should be discouraged from looking through the tools, data and
sources it indexes.

This corrects my third entry, which put "builders first".

## Personas (my inference from his three examples, not researched)

- **A farmer or landholder.** Question: what happens to my water, soil or
  income if I change what I do? Little time, likely on a phone, trusts
  neighbours and extension agents over vendors. Needs: cost, effort, and an
  example from a place like theirs.
- **A city or agency staffer doing outreach.** Question: how do I show
  residents what this proposal would do, and let them respond? Has a small
  budget and a procurement process. Needs: what similar towns did, what it
  cost, who can help.
- **A resident or community group with an issue.** Question: is the claim
  being made about this road, development or flood risk true, and is there a
  better option? No budget. Needs: free tools that run in a browser, public
  data, and a way to be taken seriously when presenting a result.
- **A builder or researcher.** Wants depth, sources and the machine-readable
  corpus. Served by the lower layer of every page.

## Design consequences

1. **Enter by situation, not taxonomy.** Front door: "I farm or manage land",
   "I work for a town or agency", "My community has a question", "I build
   these". Then the question pages. The category tree is one layer down.
2. **Two layers on every entry.** Top: what it does in one plain sentence,
   what it costs, what you need to use it, whether you can try it today, and
   an example of someone like you using it. Beneath: the technical
   description, evidence and sources. This is the house rule (plain surface,
   rigour beneath, never removed).
3. **New fields on every entry.**
   - `cost`: free / paid / price on request
   - `skill`: browser only / spreadsheet level / some code / specialist team
   - `try`: a link where it can be used right now, if one exists
   - `bring`: what data or knowledge you must supply yourself
   - `like`: a named example of who uses it
   Filters on these fields matter more to this audience than domain filters.
4. **A ladder of effort.** Look at a model someone already runs for your
   place. Use a free tool in a browser. Adapt an open model. Commission a
   build. Most people's answer is on the first two rungs, and the site should
   say so, including when a map or a spreadsheet is enough.
5. **Jargon rule.** Plain words first, the technical term after it once, and
   linked to a concept entry with a plain definition. "Digital twin" itself
   gets this treatment.
6. **Playbook for a group without specialists.** Each stage says what you can
   do yourselves, the point at which you need help, and what to ask the
   person you bring in.
7. **Conversation matters more here.** A person who does not know the
   vocabulary cannot search a catalogue. A plain question in, pointers to
   entries out, is the natural interface for this audience. The skill for a
   person's own LLM gives this at no running cost before any on-site chat.
8. **Tone.** No hype, no condescension, no dig at professionals in public
   copy. Specialists are readers too.

## A gap this exposes in the seed

The survey's catalogue is mostly tools an ordinary person cannot pick up:
utility platforms, enterprise engineering suites, national programmes. The
accessible end is thin: En-ROADS, Earth Index, Geodesignhub, Helsinki's
public atlas, InVEST. For this audience the catalogue has to grow at that
end: free mapping software, public data viewers, browser-based models,
extension-service tools. Finding and verifying those is real research and
has not been done.

## Title, reconsidered

"Digital twin" is the field's word and the search term, and it is jargon to
a farmer. Suggestion: plain words lead, the term follows.

> Model Your Place
> A public guide to digital twins of farmland, towns, watersheds and regions:
> what you can use today, what it costs, and how others have done it.

"Digital Twins of Places" stays as the runner-up. His call.
